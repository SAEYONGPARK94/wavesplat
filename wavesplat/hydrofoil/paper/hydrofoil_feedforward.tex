\begin{abstract}
We present a wave-preview feedforward controller for hydrofoil vessels
that exploits real-time spectral wave field estimation from shipboard LiDAR.
A tandem-foil hydrofoil vessel (1700~kg, 22~knots) is modeled
with 2-DOF dynamics (heave and pitch) and dual-actuator PD feedback.
The feedforward path evaluates the estimated wave field at a spatial preview
point ahead of each foil, predicting the wave-induced angle-of-attack
perturbation before it arrives.
An IIR low-pass filter ($\alpha$=0.05) on the feedforward signal suppresses
estimation noise without degrading the causal preview advantage.

Using WaveSplat---a grid-free spectral wave estimator achieving
$\sim$20\% normalized RMSE (nRMSE) at 13~ms/frame---the feedforward
controller reduces heave acceleration RMS by 11--41\% across
Beaufort~3--4 scenarios.
In noise-injection simulations, WaveSplat-level feedforward recovers
93--103\% of the perfect-knowledge benefit in Beaufort~3.
However, end-to-end testing reveals that performance is sensitive to
wave direction estimation accuracy: in Beaufort~3 the pipeline achieves
92--96\% of perfect-knowledge benefit, but in Beaufort~4 only 37\%
due to directional misestimation.
Sensitivity analysis demonstrates that feedforward remains beneficial
up to 50\% nRMSE and that the optimal preview horizon is 0.1--0.3~s
(1--3~m ahead at cruise speed).
In Beaufort~3 beam sea conditions, the end-to-end pipeline improves
passenger comfort from ISO~2631 ``Slight discomfort'' to
``Comfortable'' ($a_\text{RMS} < 0.315$~m/s$^2$).

The key finding is that even moderate wave estimation accuracy
($\sim$20\% nRMSE) is sufficient for effective feedforward control,
suggesting that LiDAR-based wave estimation is a practical enabler for
next-generation hydrofoil ride control systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The hydrofoil renaissance}

Hydrofoil vessels achieve high speed and fuel efficiency by lifting
the hull above the water surface on submerged lifting
foils, eliminating hull wave-making resistance and reducing total
drag by 40--60\% compared to displacement-hull
vessels~\citep{faltinsen2005, migeotte2001}.
Originally developed in the 1950s--1970s for military and limited
commercial applications, hydrofoils are experiencing renewed interest
driven by electric propulsion: the combination of hydrofoil efficiency
and battery-electric powertrains enables zero-emission high-speed
passenger ferry services with commercially viable
range~\citep{faltinsen2005}.
Several companies (Candela, Navier, Boundary Layer Technologies)
are now developing electric hydrofoil ferries for coastal routes,
and HD Hyundai's Avikus division is integrating autonomous navigation
with active ride control for next-generation maritime mobility.

However, the foilborne mode is inherently sensitive to wave
disturbances.
The foils operate at shallow submergence (typically 0.5--1.0~m below
the undisturbed water surface) where wave-induced
angle-of-attack (AoA) perturbations cause rapid changes in lift.
Unlike displacement hulls, which have large waterplane area providing
hydrostatic restoring forces, a foilborne vessel's vertical stability
depends entirely on the foil hydrodynamics---small AoA changes produce
large lift variations that propagate as heave and pitch accelerations
experienced by passengers.
At Beaufort~3 (the threshold for many commercial hydrofoil routes),
uncontrolled heave accelerations can exceed 1~m/s$^2$, well into the
ISO~2631 ``Fairly uncomfortable'' range~\citep{iso2631, haywood1995}.

\subsection{Limitations of pure feedback control}

The standard approach to hydrofoil ride control is active feedback:
PD or PID controllers adjust foil flap angles based on measured
heave and pitch~\citep{giron2001, esteban2005}.
This approach has been successfully deployed on commercial fast
ferries with T-foils and trim tabs~\citep{haywood1995}.

While effective at attenuating low-frequency motions, pure feedback
is fundamentally limited by the \emph{causal constraint}: the controller
can only react \emph{after} the wave disturbance has already affected
the vessel.
To quantify this limitation, consider the signal flow:
wave disturbance $\to$ foil force change $\to$ vessel acceleration
$\to$ IMU measurement $\to$ controller computation $\to$ actuator
response.
Even with fast sensors (100~Hz IMU) and actuators ($>$5~Hz bandwidth),
the total loop delay is 50--100~ms, during which the passengers
experience the full wave-induced acceleration peak.
For wave encounter periods of 1--2~s (typical for BF3 head sea at
cruise speed), this delay represents 5--10\% of the wave cycle---enough
to significantly limit the achievable acceleration reduction.

\subsection{Wave-preview feedforward}

\textbf{Feedforward} control can overcome this limitation by
pre-compensating for wave disturbances \emph{before} they reach the
foils~\citep{hrovat1997, korde2015}.
The concept is directly analogous to preview control in automotive
suspension systems, where road profile sensors ahead of the vehicle
enable proactive damper adjustment~\citep{hrovat1997}.
For hydrofoils, the ``road profile'' is the ocean wave field,
and the ``preview sensor'' is a shipboard LiDAR system.

Feedforward requires two capabilities:
(1)~real-time wave field estimation from onboard sensors, and
(2)~spatial wave preview---evaluating the estimated wave field at a point
ahead of the vessel to predict the disturbance each foil will encounter
in the near future.

The concept of wave-preview control has been explored for wave energy
converters~\citep{korde2015} and ship motion
prediction~\citep{cummins2017, belleter2015}, but practical
implementation for vessel ride control has been limited by the
difficulty of obtaining real-time, spatially resolved wave field
estimates from onboard sensors.
Recent advances in shipboard LiDAR-based wave estimation
now make this feasible.

WaveSplat \citep{park2026wavesplat} achieves real-time
spectral wave field estimation with ${\sim}20\%$ nRMSE
at 13~ms/frame from sparse LiDAR.
Its spectral representation---a weighted sum of plane
waves with known propagation---enables evaluation at
arbitrary preview points without grid interpolation.
Critically, the model provides analytic wave
slope ($\partial\eta/\partial x$) and orbital velocity
($\partial\eta/\partial t$), the disturbance quantities
needed for AoA feedforward.

\subsection{Contributions}

In this paper, we demonstrate that the LiDAR estimation-to-control
pipeline yields significant ride quality improvements.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{dual-actuator feedforward architecture} that
    decomposes wave preview into per-foil AoA compensation,
    with an IIR low-pass filter designed for noisy wave estimation
    (Section~\ref{sec:controller}).
  \item \textbf{Quantitative sensitivity analysis} showing feedforward
    tolerance to estimation error (beneficial up to 50\% nRMSE) and
    optimal preview horizon (0.1--0.3~s), providing design guidelines
    for wave estimation accuracy requirements
    (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).
  \item An \textbf{end-to-end demonstration} from LiDAR through
    WaveSplat estimation to hydrofoil feedforward, achieving
    92--96\% of perfect-knowledge performance in Beaufort~3 conditions
    and improving ISO~2631 comfort from ``Slight discomfort'' to
    ``Comfortable'' (Section~\ref{sec:e2e}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Hydrofoil dynamics and seakeeping}

The hydrodynamics of foil-borne vessels differ fundamentally from
displacement hulls.
\citet{faltinsen2005} provides a comprehensive treatment of
high-speed marine vehicle dynamics, including hydrofoil configurations.
The key characteristic is that foilborne stability depends entirely
on the lift-slope characteristics of the submerged foils: the
restoring force comes from the change in foil lift with AoA
(rather than from hydrostatic waterplane area), making the system
highly responsive to wave disturbances but also amenable to
active control.

The \emph{wave encounter frequency} is a critical parameter for
ride control design.  For a vessel traveling at speed $V$ against
waves with frequency $\omega_0$ and heading angle $\beta$ relative
to the wave direction, the encountered frequency
is~\citep{faltinsen2005}:
\begin{equation}
  \omega_e = \omega_0 - \frac{\omega_0^2}{g} V \cos\beta
  \label{eq:encounter}
\end{equation}
For head seas ($\beta = 180^\circ$) at 22~knots against BF3 waves
($T_p = 5$~s, $\omega_0 = 1.26$~rad/s), the encounter frequency is
$\omega_e \approx 3.1$~rad/s ($T_e \approx 2.0$~s), more than doubling the
apparent wave frequency.
This Doppler effect means that hydrofoil ride control must handle
higher effective frequencies than the ambient sea state suggests.

\subsection{Active ride control for high-speed vessels}

Active ride control systems for high-speed marine vessels have been
studied extensively since the 1990s.
\citet{haywood1995} developed a ride control system for fast ferries
(40--50~m class) using T-foils and trim tabs with PID feedback,
demonstrating 30--50\% motion reduction in head seas and establishing
that ride control is essential for commercial viability of fast ferry
services.
\citet{giron2001} demonstrated multi-actuator
control combining fins and T-foils for motion stabilization of fast
ferries, introducing the collective--differential control decomposition
that separates heave and pitch control channels.
\citet{esteban2005} modeled fast ship dynamics with
flaps and T-foils using strip theory, showing that pitch control
requires differential actuation between fore and aft control surfaces
due to the coupled nature of heave-pitch dynamics.
\citet{broglia2014} investigated hydrodynamic interference effects
for high-speed catamarans, highlighting the importance of accurate
force modeling including free-surface effects near the foils.

All these works use pure feedback control; none exploit
wave preview information for feedforward compensation.
The feedback-only limitation is particularly acute for hydrofoils,
where the high encounter frequencies and low system damping create
sharp acceleration peaks that feedback cannot attenuate.

\subsection{Model-predictive control for marine vessels}

Model-predictive control (MPC) has been applied to marine vessel
motion control in several contexts.
\citet{perez2012} developed MPC-based ship roll stabilization
using fins, demonstrating constraint handling and multi-objective
optimization.
For wave energy converters, MPC with wave preview information
has achieved near-optimal energy extraction~\citep{korde2015}.
However, MPC for hydrofoil ride control has not been demonstrated,
partly due to the high update rates required (100~Hz for foilborne
dynamics) and the computational cost of online optimization.

Our feedforward approach can be viewed as a simplified alternative
to MPC that trades optimality for computational simplicity: rather
than solving an online optimization problem, we apply a proportional
gain to the predicted disturbance with low-pass filtering.
This simplification is justified by the approximately linear
relationship between wave-induced AoA and optimal flap correction
in the operating regime considered.

\subsection{Wave feedforward and preview control}

The concept of using ahead-of-vessel wave measurements for
feedforward control has its roots in automotive preview
suspension~\citep{hrovat1997}, where road profile sensors
(cameras, LiDAR) ahead of the vehicle enable proactive damper
adjustment.
In the maritime domain, this concept has been explored primarily
for wave energy converters and ship motion prediction.
\citet{korde2015} showed that deterministic wave prediction enables
near-optimal power extraction through preview-based control.
\citet{cummins2017} demonstrated that wave preview
from radar or LiDAR can improve short-term ship motion prediction,
and \citet{belleter2015} developed observers for wave encounter
frequency estimation from ship motion measurements.

The integration of real-time wave field estimation with
vessel motion control has not been demonstrated for hydrofoil
vessels, despite the strong physical motivation: the shallow foil
submergence creates a direct, nearly linear coupling between wave
elevation/slope and foil forces that is particularly amenable to
feedforward compensation.

\subsection{LiDAR-based wave field estimation}

Shipboard scanning LiDAR provides direct ocean surface elevation
measurements at 10~Hz frame rate~\citep{nouguier2014, collins2022}.
Grid-based methods using the Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter
(ETKF)~\citep{huhn2022, desmars2020} achieve accurate
reconstruction but require spatial discretization and grid interpolation.
WaveSplat~\citep{park2026wavesplat} provides a grid-free spectral
alternative with $\sim$20\% nRMSE and 13~ms/frame inference.
Its analytic wave model enables direct evaluation of surface
elevation, slope, and orbital velocity at arbitrary spatial points,
making it naturally suited for the preview-point evaluation required
by feedforward control.

\section{Hydrofoil Dynamics Model}
\label{sec:dynamics}

\subsection{Reference vessel}

We model a representative small electric hydrofoil passenger
vessel with parameters within the range of emerging coastal
hydrofoil ferries~\citep{faltinsen2005}:
LOA~8.5~m, beam~2.4~m, displacement~1700~kg, cruising at
22~knots (11.3~m/s) in foilborne mode with a tandem hydrofoil
configuration (front inverted-T foil, rear T-foil).
Structural damping and added mass coefficients are set to
values typical for small foilborne craft in which hull
emergence reduces hydrodynamic added mass~\citep{faltinsen2005}.

\subsection{Two-DOF equations of motion}

The vessel state is $\mathbf{x} = [z,\, \theta,\, \dot{z},\, \dot{\theta}]^T$
where $z$ is heave (positive up) and $\theta$ is pitch (positive bow-up).
The equations of motion are:
\begin{align}
  (m + a_{33})\,\ddot{z} &= L_f + L_r - mg - B_z \dot{z}
  \label{eq:heave} \\
  (I_{yy} + a_{55})\,\ddot{\theta} &= L_f x_f + L_r x_r - B_\theta \dot{\theta}
  \label{eq:pitch}
\end{align}
where $L_f$, $L_r$ are lift forces on front and rear foils,
$x_f = 2.0$~m and $x_r = -2.5$~m are foil positions relative to
the center of gravity, $a_{33} = 0.05m$ and $a_{55} = 0.05 I_{yy}$
are added mass terms (small in foilborne mode due to hull emergence),
and $B_z = 50$~N$\cdot$s/m, $B_\theta = 20$~N$\cdot$m$\cdot$s/rad
are structural damping coefficients.

\textbf{Justification for 2-DOF model.}
In foilborne mode, heave and pitch are the dominant degrees of freedom
affecting passenger comfort: the ISO~2631 comfort metric is based
on vertical acceleration, and pitch directly modulates foil AoA.
Roll is excluded because
(1)~the symmetric tandem-foil configuration provides natural roll
    restoring moment through differential lift, and
(2)~at the cruise speeds considered (22~knots), roll response to
    beam seas is an order of magnitude smaller than heave response
    for vessels of this size~\citep{faltinsen2005}.
Surge, sway, and yaw are controlled by propulsion and rudder systems
and decouple from the ride control problem at first order.

\subsection{Foil hydrodynamics}

Each foil produces lift proportional to its effective angle of attack:
\begin{equation}
  L_i = \tfrac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S_i \, C_{L,i}(\alpha_{\text{eff},i}) \cdot \sigma(h_i)
  \label{eq:lift}
\end{equation}
where $S_i = b_i c_i$ is the planform area,
$C_{L,i} = C_{L\alpha,i}(\alpha_{\text{eff},i} - \alpha_{zl,i})$
is the lift coefficient (clamped at $\pm C_{L,\max}$=1.2 to model stall),
and $\sigma(h_i) \in [0, 1]$ is a free-surface proximity correction
that accounts for ventilation risk near the water surface:
$\sigma = 1$ for $h/c \geq 2$ (fully submerged),
linearly ramping to $\sigma = 0$ at $h = 0$ (surface breach).
Submergence $h_i$ is computed from design depth, heave, pitch
geometry, and local wave elevation.

Both foils use NACA~63-series sections, whose hydrodynamic
characteristics are well-documented~\citep{abbott1959}.
The front foil (inverted-T, $b$=1.8~m, $c$=0.20~m,
$C_{L\alpha} = 2\pi \times 0.85 \approx 5.34$~rad$^{-1}$ after
finite-span correction for AR=9)
carries $\sim$56\% of the vessel weight; the rear foil (T-foil,
$b$=1.2~m, $c$=0.15~m,
$C_{L\alpha} = 2\pi \times 0.80 \approx 5.03$~rad$^{-1}$, AR=8)
carries the remainder.
The load split is determined by moment balance through the design
zero-lift angles $\alpha_{zl}$.
Table~\ref{tab:foil_params} summarizes the foil parameters.

\subsection{Effective angle of attack}

The effective AoA at each foil combines five contributions:
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{\text{eff}} = \theta
    \underbrace{- \frac{\dot{z}}{V}}_{\text{heave rate}}
    \underbrace{- \frac{\dot{\theta} x_{\text{foil}}}{V}}_{\text{pitch rate}}
    \underbrace{- \frac{w}{V}}_{\text{orbital vel.}}
    \underbrace{- \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial x_b}}_{\text{wave slope}}
  \label{eq:alpha_eff}
\end{equation}
where $w = \partial\eta/\partial t$ is the wave orbital velocity and
$\partial\eta/\partial x_b$ is the wave slope projected onto the
vessel's body-frame heading axis:
$\partial\eta/\partial x_b = (\partial\eta/\partial x)\cos\psi + (\partial\eta/\partial y)\sin\psi$.
The last two terms are the wave disturbance that the feedforward
controller aims to pre-compensate.
The first three terms depend on vessel state and are handled by PD
feedback.

\subsection{Wave disturbance model}

The wave field is a superposition of $N$ linear plane waves:
\begin{equation}
  \eta(x, y, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} A_i \cos(k_{x,i} x + k_{y,i} y - \omega_i t + \varphi_i)
  \label{eq:wave}
\end{equation}
with angular frequency constrained by the dispersion
relation~\citep{holthuijsen2007}:
\begin{equation}
  \omega_i = \sqrt{g\,|\mathbf{k}_i|\,\tanh(|\mathbf{k}_i|\,d)}.
  \label{eq:dispersion}
\end{equation}
Wave components are sampled from a JONSWAP
spectrum~\citep{hasselmann1973} with directional spreading
($\pm 20^\circ$).
Slope and orbital velocity are computed analytically
from~\eqref{eq:wave}, enabling evaluation at arbitrary
spatial points---a key advantage of the spectral
representation for feedforward preview.

The disturbance at each foil is computed by evaluating~\eqref{eq:wave}
at the foil's instantaneous global position
$x_{\text{foil}} = x_{\text{CG}}(t) + x_i \cos\psi$,
where $\psi$ is the vessel heading.

\subsection{Numerical integration}

The system is integrated with the classical 4th-order Runge-Kutta method
at $\Delta t = 0.01$~s (100~Hz).
A critical implementation detail: the feedforward signal is low-pass
filtered \emph{once} per main timestep and passed as a \emph{fixed offset}
to all four RK4 sub-steps, while PD feedback is recomputed at each
sub-step from the current state.
This prevents the IIR filter from advancing multiple times per
timestep---a subtle numerical issue that would cause over-filtering and
significantly reduce the feedforward benefit.

\section{Controller Architecture}
\label{sec:controller}

\subsection{PD feedback (baseline)}

The baseline controller uses collective--differential
decomposition~\citep{giron2001}:
\begin{align}
  \delta_c &= -K_{p,z}\, z - K_{d,z}\, \dot{z}
  \label{eq:collective} \\
  \delta_d &= K_{p,\theta}\, \theta + K_{d,\theta}\, \dot{\theta}
  \label{eq:differential}
\end{align}
with flap commands $\delta_f = \delta_c - \delta_d$ (front) and
$\delta_r = \delta_c + \delta_d$ (rear), saturated at
$\pm \delta_{\max} = 0.26$~rad ($\pm 15^\circ$).
The gains are tuned based on dimensional analysis of the foil
force sensitivity.
The heave proportional gain $K_{p,z}=0.8$ is set such that a
1~cm heave displacement produces $\sim$0.5$^\circ$ flap deflection,
sufficient to generate the corrective lift change
$\Delta L \approx \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S C_{L\alpha} K_{p,z} \Delta z
\approx 150$~N for $\Delta z = 0.01$~m.
The derivative gains ($K_{d,z}=0.3$, $K_{d,\theta}=1.5$) provide
damping at approximately 0.3--0.4 of critical damping.
The pitch proportional gain $K_{p,\theta}=4.0$ is set high to
ensure pitch stability (see below).
All gains are held constant across sea states to demonstrate
robustness rather than optimal per-condition tuning.

The high pitch-to-heave gain ratio ($K_{p,\theta}/K_{p,z} = 5$) reflects
a fundamental stability consideration: with tandem foils separated
by 4.5~m, pitch is naturally unstable---front-foil lift perturbations
create bow-up moments that increase front-foil AoA further.
Pitch stability must be prioritized over heave regulation.

Actuators are modeled as ideal (instantaneous response).
This assumption is justified because typical hydraulic foil flap
actuators achieve bandwidth $>$5~Hz~\citep{haywood1995},
well above the feedforward signal bandwidth ($<$1~Hz for the
wave encounter frequencies considered).

\subsection{Wave-preview feedforward}

The feedforward controller evaluates the wave field at a
\emph{spatial preview point} ahead of each foil:
\begin{equation}
  x_{\text{preview},i} = x_{\text{foil},i} + V \cdot \Delta t_{\text{prev}}
  \label{eq:preview}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t_{\text{prev}}$ is the preview time horizon
(0.5~s $\approx$ 5.65~m ahead at 11.3~m/s for the baseline evaluation;
Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} optimizes this to 0.1--0.3~s).

At this preview point, the wave slope $\partial\eta/\partial x$ and
orbital velocity $w$ are evaluated at the \emph{current} time $t$.
The spatial preview exploits the fact that the wave field ahead of
the vessel is observable now (via LiDAR estimation) and will propagate
to the foil location in $\Delta t_\text{prev}$ seconds.
The predicted wave-induced AoA perturbation is:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\alpha}_{\text{wave},i} = -\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}\bigg|_{\text{prev},i}
    - \frac{w|_{\text{prev},i}}{V}
  \label{eq:alpha_preview}
\end{equation}

The feedforward flap command counteracts this prediction:
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{\text{ff},i} = -K_{\text{ff}} \cdot \hat{\alpha}_{\text{wave},i}
  \label{eq:ff_command}
\end{equation}
with gain $K_{\text{ff}} = 0.8$ (80\% compensation, providing
robustness margin against estimation error and model uncertainty).

\subsection{Low-pass filtering for noisy estimation}

When using estimated (noisy) wave fields, the feedforward signal
contains high-frequency estimation noise that can excite structural
modes and degrade actuator lifetime.
We apply a first-order IIR low-pass filter:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\delta}_{\text{ff}}[n] = \alpha \cdot \delta_{\text{ff}}[n]
    + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \bar{\delta}_{\text{ff}}[n-1]
  \label{eq:lpf}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 0.05$, giving a cutoff frequency of
$f_c = \alpha/(2\pi\Delta t) \approx 0.8$~Hz at $\Delta t = 0.01$~s.
The filter parameter $\alpha$ is chosen so that $f_c$ exceeds the
maximum wave encounter frequency across all scenarios
($f_e \leq 0.5$~Hz for BF3 head sea at 22~knots), ensuring
the wave preview signal passes through while high-frequency
estimation noise is suppressed.

The total flap command is:
\begin{equation}
  \delta_i = \underbrace{\delta_c \pm \delta_d}_{\text{PD feedback}}
    + \underbrace{\bar{\delta}_{\text{ff},i}}_{\text{filtered FF}}
  \label{eq:total_flap}
\end{equation}

\subsection{WaveSplat integration}

WaveSplat~\citep{park2026wavesplat} is a grid-free spectral wave
estimation method that processes sparse LiDAR surface observations
(50--200 points/frame at 10~Hz) through a two-phase pipeline:
(1)~dispersion-aware beamforming for spectral initialization
(wavenumber extraction), followed by
(2)~per-frame gradient-based tracking that updates amplitudes and
phases while freezing wavenumbers.
The estimated wave field is:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\eta}(x,y,t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cos(k_{x,i} x + k_{y,i} y - \omega_i t + \varphi_i)
\end{equation}
with $\omega_i$ constrained by the dispersion relation, achieving
$\sim$20\% nRMSE at 13~ms/frame on CPU.

This spectral representation allows \emph{direct} analytical evaluation of
$\hat{\eta}$, $\partial\hat{\eta}/\partial x$, and
$\partial\hat{\eta}/\partial t$ at any spatial point without
grid interpolation---a key enabler for the spatial
preview required by feedforward control.

The WaveSplat-to-controller bridge maps the estimated spectral
parameters $(w_i, k_{x,i}, k_{y,i}, \omega_i, \varphi_i)$ to
wave components compatible with the disturbance model~\eqref{eq:wave}.
The bridge handles WaveSplat's signed amplitudes ($w_i$ can be
negative, equivalent to a $\pi$ phase shift) transparently.

\section{Wave-Foil Interaction Analysis}
\label{sec:wavefoi}

Before presenting the experimental setup, we analyze the physical
mechanism by which waves disturb the hydrofoil vessel, motivating the
feedforward controller design.

\subsection{Dominant disturbance channels}

From the effective AoA expression~\eqref{eq:alpha_eff}, the wave
disturbance enters through two channels:
(1)~wave slope $\partial\eta/\partial x_b$, which directly tilts
the inflow direction relative to the foil chord; and
(2)~orbital velocity $w = \partial\eta/\partial t$, which adds a
vertical component to the inflow velocity.
For a single plane wave $\eta = A\cos(kx - \omega t)$, these
contributions are:
\begin{align}
  \frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}\bigg|_\text{foil} &= -Ak\sin(kx_f - \omega t)
  \label{eq:slope_single} \\
  \frac{w}{V}\bigg|_\text{foil} &= \frac{A\omega}{V}\sin(kx_f - \omega t)
  \label{eq:orbital_single}
\end{align}
Both terms are proportional to $A$ and oscillate at the encounter
frequency.  Their relative magnitude is:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{|w/V|}{|\partial\eta/\partial x|} = \frac{\omega}{kV}
    = \frac{c_p}{V}
\end{equation}
where $c_p = \omega/k$ is the phase velocity.
For BF3 conditions ($T_p = 5$~s, $c_p \approx 7.8$~m/s) at
$V = 11.3$~m/s, this ratio is $\sim$0.69, meaning the wave slope
and orbital velocity contributions are comparable.
At higher sea states (BF4, $T_p = 7.5$~s, $c_p \approx 11.7$~m/s),
the orbital velocity dominates ($c_p/V \approx 1.04$).

This analysis has two implications for feedforward design:
(1)~both slope and orbital velocity must be estimated and
    compensated, as neither dominates across all conditions; and
(2)~the feedforward controller gains should not depend strongly on
    sea state, since the disturbance structure (relative weighting
    of slope vs.\ orbital velocity) changes with wave period.

\subsection{Encounter frequency and preview}

The encounter frequency~\eqref{eq:encounter} determines how much
time the feedforward controller has to react.
Table~\ref{tab:encounter} shows the encounter period for the four
scenarios.

\begin{tabular}{lcccc}

Scenario & $T_e$ [s] & $f_e$ [Hz] & $\lambda$ [m] & $\lambda/L$ \\
\midrule
BF3 Head    & 2.0 & 0.49 & 39 & 4.6 \\

attenuation.
Beam seas have the lowest encounter frequency (0.20~Hz, equal to the

For all scenarios, $\lambda/L > 4$, meaning the wave field is nearly

\subsection{Control strategies}

We compare four control strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{PD-only}: feedback baseline (no wave knowledge)
  \item \textbf{PD + GT FF}: feedforward with perfect wave knowledge
    (theoretical upper bound)
  \item \textbf{PD + WaveSplat FF}: feedforward with $\sim$20\% nRMSE
    estimation noise ($\sigma_\eta = 0.20 H_s$,
    $\sigma_{\text{slope}} = 0.02$~rad, additive white Gaussian)
  \item \textbf{PD + Noisy FF}: feedforward with $\sim$40\% nRMSE
    (degraded estimation, $\sigma_\eta = 0.40 H_s$,
    $\sigma_{\text{slope}} = 0.05$~rad)
\end{enumerate}

Note: Strategies~3--4 simulate estimation error via additive white
Gaussian noise, which is a conservative model---real WaveSplat
errors are temporally correlated (tracking drift) and may contain
structured biases (spectral leakage), potentially making them
\emph{easier} to filter.
The end-to-end demonstration (Section~\ref{sec:e2e}) validates
performance under realistic WaveSplat error characteristics.

\subsection{Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Heave RMS} ($z_\text{RMS}$): standard deviation of heave [cm]
  \item \textbf{Acceleration RMS} ($a_\text{RMS}$): standard deviation of heave
    acceleration [m/s$^2$]---the primary comfort metric
  \item \textbf{Comfort rating}: ISO~2631-1~\citep{iso2631} classification
    based on $a_\text{RMS}$:
    Comfortable ($<$0.315~m/s$^2$),
    Slight discomfort (0.315--0.63~m/s$^2$),
    Fairly uncomfortable (0.63--1.0~m/s$^2$)
  \item \textbf{Improvement}: $\Delta = (1 - \text{metric}_\text{FF} / \text{metric}_\text{PD}) \times 100\%$---percentage reduction relative to PD-only
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Estimation error tolerance}

We sweep the wave estimation error (nRMSE) from 0\% (GT) to
50\% to determine the degradation threshold
(Fig.~\ref{fig:nrmse}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../figures/fig_nrmse_sensitivity.pdf}

\label{fig:nrmse}
\end{figure}

\textbf{No degradation threshold was found}: feedforward improves

This is because the LP filter ($\alpha$=0.05) attenuates high-frequency

The improvement degrades monotonically with increasing nRMSE, but
never crosses zero within the tested range.

This result has important practical implications: it means that
wave estimation systems do not need to achieve very high accuracy
to be useful for feedforward control.
Even a coarse wave field estimate ($\sim$50\% nRMSE) provides
positive benefit after LP filtering, lowering the bar for
practical deployment.

\subsection{Preview distance optimization}

Figure~\ref{fig:preview} shows the acceleration improvement
as a function of the preview time horizon $\Delta t_\text{prev}$.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../figures/fig_preview_sweep.pdf}
\caption{Feedforward improvement vs.\ preview distance for GT
(solid) and WaveSplat (dashed). Stars mark optimal preview.
Short preview (0.1--0.3~s) is optimal; longer horizons degrade

\label{fig:preview}

The optimal preview is surprisingly short:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Head sea}: 0.1~s optimal ($\sim$1.1~m ahead)---the

    22~knots against BF3 waves) means the relevant wave features are

  \item \textbf{Beam sea}: 0.3~s optimal ($\sim$3.4~m ahead)---lower

    a longer useful preview window.
\end{itemize}

Longer preview distances degrade performance because the spatial
wave field decorrelates over distance: the wave pattern at 20~m
ahead (2~s preview) may have evolved significantly by the time the
vessel arrives due to wave dispersion and multi-component
interference.
With noisy estimation (WaveSplat), the optimal preview shifts
slightly shorter, as estimation error accumulates more at distant
preview points where LiDAR accuracy is lower.

These results suggest that the preview advantage of feedforward
control for hydrofoils is fundamentally limited by the spatial
scale of wave-foil interaction (chord-scale, $\sim$0.2~m) rather
than by the preview distance itself.

\section{End-to-End Demonstration}
\label{sec:e2e}

To validate the full pipeline under realistic conditions, we run
WaveSplat estimation on simulated LiDAR episodes and feed the
estimated spectral parameters directly into the hydrofoil
feedforward controller.

\subsection{Pipeline architecture}

The end-to-end pipeline operates as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item LiDAR scans provide sparse ocean surface observations
    (50--200 points/frame at 10~Hz)
  \item WaveSplat estimates spectral parameters
    $(w_i, k_{x,i}, k_{y,i}, \omega_i, \varphi_i)$ in 13~ms/frame
  \item The WaveSplat bridge converts these to wave components
    compatible with the disturbance model
  \item The hydrofoil simulator uses GT components for
    \emph{disturbance} (representing physical reality) and WaveSplat
    components for \emph{feedforward} (representing the controller's
    imperfect knowledge)
\end{enumerate}

This ``split'' simulation faithfully models the realistic scenario:
the ocean produces forces based on the true wave field, while the
controller can only act on what WaveSplat has estimated from
LiDAR observations.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why feedforward works for hydrofoils}

The fundamental advantage of feedforward control is \emph{causal
preview}: the wave field ahead of the vessel is observable
(via LiDAR estimation) before it reaches the foils.
This breaks the fundamental bandwidth limitation of feedback control,
where the controller can only react after the disturbance has already
produced measurable vessel motion.

Even with 20\% estimation error, the low-frequency wave slope
and orbital velocity---which drive the dominant AoA
perturbation---are captured with sufficient accuracy for effective
pre-compensation.
This is because the feedforward signal acts on the
\emph{wave-frequency} content of the disturbance ($<$1~Hz), while
estimation errors are predominantly at higher frequencies (noise)
or lower amplitudes (spectral leakage), both of which are
effectively suppressed by the LP filter.

Hydrofoils are particularly well-suited for wave feedforward
compared to other vessel types because:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The foils operate at \emph{fixed depth}, creating a direct and
    nearly linear coupling between wave elevation/slope at the foil
    position and the resulting lift force.
    For displacement hulls, wave-body interaction involves complex
    diffraction and radiation effects that are harder to predict from
    wave field information alone.
  \item The high cruise speed (22~knots) provides \emph{natural spatial
    preview}---the wave field observed at a given spatial point will
    arrive at the foil within a fraction of a second.
    This is analogous to automotive preview suspension, where higher
    vehicle speed provides longer preview time for a given sensor range.
  \item The foilborne dynamics have \emph{relatively low damping},
    meaning that uncompensated wave disturbances persist as oscillations
    rather than being quickly dissipated.
    Feedback can attenuate these oscillations but cannot prevent the
    initial acceleration peak; feedforward can.
  \item The spectral representation of the wave field enables
    \emph{analytic evaluation} of slope and orbital velocity at any
    spatial point, eliminating the interpolation error that would
    arise from grid-based estimation methods.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Comparison with feedback-only performance}

The 11--41\% acceleration reduction achieved by feedforward
(Table~\ref{tab:phase2}) should be interpreted in the context of
the already-effective PD feedback baseline.
The PD controller already reduces heave acceleration significantly
compared to an uncontrolled vessel; the feedforward improvement
is on top of this baseline.
In beam seas, where the wave disturbance has a strong persistent
component aligned with the foil's lateral axis, feedforward achieves
a 41\% \emph{additional} reduction---suggesting that beam-sea
conditions are where the feedforward benefit is most impactful for
operational regularity.

\subsection{LP filter design}

The choice $\alpha=0.05$ (cutoff $\sim$0.8~Hz) is critical for
robust performance with noisy estimation.
Preliminary tests with $\alpha=0.15$ (weaker filtering) allowed
excessive noise to pass through, degrading beam/quarter sea
performance by up to 40\%.
The aggressive $\alpha=0.05$ setting is viable because the
relevant wave encounter frequencies ($\sim$0.49~Hz for BF3 head
sea, $\sim$0.20~Hz for beam sea) remain well below the cutoff.

\paragraph{Sensitivity and honest reporting of sub-optimality.}
A sweep $\alpha\in\{0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20\}$ on the
four representative configurations (Table~\ref{tab:lpalpha}) shows
that $\alpha=0.05$ is near-optimal for three of the four cases
(BF3~Head, BF3~Beam, BF4~Beam: within 2--3~percentage points of the
best acceleration reduction observed across all $\alpha$), but
\emph{sub-optimal} for BF4~Head, where the best acceleration
reduction ($+13.7\%$) is attained at $\alpha=0.01$ and degrades
monotonically as $\alpha$ grows, crossing zero at $\alpha\approx 0.10$
and becoming strictly harmful for $\alpha\geq 0.15$.  We keep
$\alpha=0.05$ in the main results because (i)~it is the
best-single-value choice across the four cases jointly and (ii)~it
matches the design frequency derived from BF3 encounter.  However,
this transparently implies that the BF4~Head degradation reported
in Section~\ref{sec:results} (WS/GT $=37\%$) is in part a filter-tuning
artifact on top of the directional estimation bottleneck, and that a
sea-state-adaptive $\alpha$ schedule is a concrete path to improvement
that does not require any change to the wave estimator itself.

The pre-filtered feedforward architecture (filtering once per
timestep, before RK4 sub-steps) is essential: if the IIR filter
advanced at each RK4 sub-step, it would effectively use
$\alpha_\text{eff} = 1-(1-\alpha)^4 \approx 4\alpha = 0.20$,
drastically reducing the useful signal.

\subsection{Preview horizon}

The surprisingly short optimal preview (0.1--0.3~s) reflects two
competing effects: (1)~longer preview provides more anticipation
time for the control system to respond, but (2)~the spatial
wave field decorrelates over distance due to wave dispersion.
At cruise speed (11.3~m/s), even 0.1~s preview provides 1.1~m
of spatial anticipation---sufficient for the foil chord-scale
($\sim$0.2~m) AoA variations that dominate the disturbance.

This finding is practically important: it means that the LiDAR
estimation system needs to be accurate primarily in the near-field
(within $\sim$3~m of each foil in the heading direction), where
LiDAR accuracy is typically highest.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{2-DOF simplification}: The model neglects roll,
    which may be significant in beam seas for vessels with
    asymmetric foil configurations.  Extension to 3-DOF (adding roll)
    is straightforward and would enable beam-sea roll control via
    differential port/starboard foil actuation.
  \item \textbf{Ideal actuators}: Real foil flap actuators have finite
    bandwidth ($\sim$5--10~Hz) and rate limits.
    While the feedforward signal bandwidth ($<$1~Hz) is well below
    typical actuator bandwidth, actuator saturation during combined
    feedback+feedforward operation could limit performance in rough seas.
  \item \textbf{Simulation-only validation}: All results are obtained
    in simulation with a simplified vessel model.  Validation on a
    physical hydrofoil testbed is essential to confirm the practical
    benefits, particularly regarding sensor integration, actuator
    dynamics, and real sea state variability.
  \item \textbf{Directional estimation}: The BF4 degradation (WS/GT=37\%)
    highlights that directional estimation accuracy is a critical
    bottleneck.  This is consistent with the pre-registered six-metric
    evaluation in the companion paper \citep{park2026wavesplat}, where
    WaveSplat attains a directional $R^2$ of $-0.25$ on the 2D
    wavenumber spectrum across 100 episodes: propagation direction
    of individual spectral components is worse than chance on average,
    even though the nRMSE on the elevation field is the lowest among
    the evaluated methods.  For head-seas operation, where the
    feedforward signal is a 1-D projection along the ship heading,
    a random directional error translates into a cosine-modulated
    phase misalignment that the IIR low-pass filter cannot recover.
    Directional confidence weighting or gating in the feedforward
    signal could mitigate this without requiring perfect directional
    estimation.
  \item \textbf{Simulation duration}: The 30-second episodes, while
    sufficient for demonstrating the feedforward concept, are
    shorter than typical operational durations.  Longer simulations
    (60--120~s) would provide more statistically robust comfort
    metrics and better characterize tracking stability.
  \item \textbf{Vessel parameter sensitivity}: The simulation uses a
    single representative vessel configuration.  Structural damping
    coefficients and hydrodynamic derivatives are set to typical
    values from the literature~\citep{faltinsen2005} but may differ
    for specific hull-foil designs.
    Since the feedforward architecture operates on the estimated
    wave disturbance rather than on vessel-specific dynamics, the
    qualitative conclusions (feedforward beneficial up to 50\% nRMSE,
    short optimal preview) are expected to generalize, though
    quantitative acceleration reductions will vary with vessel parameters.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We demonstrated that LiDAR-based spectral wave estimation can be
directly exploited for feedforward ride control of hydrofoil vessels.
The key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Wave-preview feedforward reduces heave acceleration by
    11--41\% with perfect wave knowledge, and 11--38\% with
    WaveSplat estimation ($\sim$20\% nRMSE), on top of an already-effective
    PD feedback baseline.
  \item The feedforward is robust to estimation error: no degradation
    threshold was found up to 50\% nRMSE, thanks to IIR low-pass
    filtering ($\alpha$=0.05).  This means that wave estimation systems
    do not need to achieve very high accuracy to provide meaningful
    ride quality improvement.
  \item Optimal preview is short (0.1--0.3~s), matching the spatial
    scale of wave-foil interaction rather than vessel-scale preview.
    This is advantageous because LiDAR accuracy is highest in the
    near field where the preview points are located.
  \item End-to-end, WaveSplat feedforward achieves 92--96\% of
    perfect-knowledge performance in BF3, improving ISO~2631 comfort
    from ``Slight discomfort'' to ``Comfortable''---a practically
    meaningful improvement for commercial passenger operations.
  \item Directional estimation accuracy is more critical than amplitude
    accuracy for feedforward performance, as evidenced by the
    BF4 degradation (WS/GT = 37\%).  This finding provides a clear
    design target for wave estimation system improvement.
\end{enumerate}

These results establish that even moderate wave estimation accuracy
($\sim$20\% nRMSE) is sufficient for effective feedforward control.
The total computational budget for the LiDAR $\to$ WaveSplat $\to$
feedforward pipeline is 14.6~ms per cycle (Table~\ref{tab:latency}:
13.4~ms estimation + 1.2~ms preprocessing, controller, and I/O),
well within the 100~ms real-time budget on standard shipboard
computing hardware.
This makes the approach a practical candidate for deployment on
the emerging class of electric hydrofoil passenger vessels, where
ride comfort directly impacts commercial viability.

\textbf{Roadmap for future work.}
The natural next steps are:
(1)~extending the dynamics model to 6-DOF with roll, which enables
    beam-sea roll control via differential port/starboard foil actuation;
(2)~replacing the proportional feedforward with model-predictive
    control (MPC) that can handle actuator constraints ($\pm 15^\circ$
    flap limits) and optimize over a receding horizon, potentially
    improving performance in rough seas where actuator saturation
    is a concern;
(3)~longer simulation campaigns (60--120~s) with multiple random
    seeds for statistically robust comfort assessment;
(4)~directional confidence weighting in the feedforward gain,
    reducing the feedforward authority for components with uncertain
    propagation direction; and
(5)~validation on a physical hydrofoil testbed to confirm that the
    simulated benefits translate to measurable comfort improvement
    under real sea conditions, sensor noise, and actuator dynamics.
