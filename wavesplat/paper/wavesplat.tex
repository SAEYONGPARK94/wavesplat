\title{WaveSplat: Grid-Free Spectral Gaussian Wave Field Estimation from Sparse LiDAR}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Saeyong Shin}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Avikus (HD Hyundai) \\
saeyong.shin@avikus.ai}}

\begin{abstract}
We present WaveSplat, a grid-free method for real-time ocean wave field
estimation from sparse shipboard LiDAR observations.
Unlike grid-based approaches such as the Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter (ETKF),
WaveSplat represents the wave field as a weighted sum of spectral Gaussian
components---plane waves parameterized by wavenumber, amplitude, and phase---with
angular frequency constrained by the linear dispersion relation.
This grid-free representation naturally avoids interpolation artifacts,
scales to arbitrary spatial resolution, and enables an adaptive number of
wave components matched to scene complexity.
We propose a two-phase estimation pipeline: (1) initialization via
dispersion-aware beamforming with coarse-to-fine refinement, and
(2) per-frame tracking with physics-based phase prediction and selective
gradient updates.
Fresnel-zone confidence weighting accounts for range- and angle-dependent
LiDAR measurement noise.
On 12 representative episodes spanning Beaufort 2--5 sea states
(1--30 wave components), WaveSplat achieves an average nRMSE of 0.211
compared to 0.436 for ETKF-grid (51.5\% improvement), while running
2.4$\times$ faster (13.4 vs.\ 31.7~ms/frame).
With adaptive $N$, the model automatically selects $N$=1 for simple swells
and $N$=21 for complex multi-component seas.
\end{abstract}

Wave field estimation, LiDAR, spectral methods, Gaussian splatting,

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Accurate estimation of the ocean wave field around a vessel is critical for
autonomous ship operations, including motion prediction, route optimization,
and structural load monitoring~\cite{desmars2020}.
Shipboard scanning LiDAR has emerged as a practical sensor for this task,
providing direct elevation measurements over a wide area at high frame
rates ($\sim$10~Hz)~\cite{nouguier2014}.
However, the observations are inherently sparse (typically 500--2000 points
per scan), unevenly distributed (denser near the ship), and noisy
(accuracy degrades with range and incidence angle).

The dominant approach in the literature follows a grid-based paradigm:
discretize the spatial domain on a regular grid, represent the wave state
as surface elevation and velocity on this grid, and apply an ensemble
Kalman filter (EnKF/ETKF) to assimilate observations~\cite{desmars2020,
huhn2022}.
While principled, this approach has three fundamental limitations:
(1) \textbf{grid interpolation}: observations must be interpolated to grid
points, introducing smoothing and aliasing for wavelengths near the grid
Nyquist limit;
(2) \textbf{fixed resolution}: the grid resolution is a design parameter
that trades accuracy against computational cost, with no adaptation to the
actual wave complexity;
(3) \textbf{ensemble collapse}: when the number of observations far exceeds
the ensemble size ($M \gg N_e$), the ETKF analysis can become rank-deficient,
requiring observation thinning or localization.

We propose \textbf{WaveSplat}, a grid-free spectral representation inspired
by 3D Gaussian Splatting~\cite{kerbl2023}.
Instead of discretizing space, we represent the wave field as a weighted
superposition of plane waves:
\begin{equation}
  z(x, y, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cos(k_{x,i} x + k_{y,i} y - \omega_i t + \varphi_i)
  \label{eq:forward}
\end{equation}
where each ``spectral Gaussian'' is parameterized by amplitude $w_i$,
wavenumber $(k_{x,i}, k_{y,i})$, and phase $\varphi_i$.
The angular frequency $\omega_i$ is not a free parameter but is
\emph{constrained} by the linear dispersion relation:
\begin{equation}
  \omega_i = \sqrt{g \, |k_i| \, \tanh(|k_i| \, d)}
  \label{eq:dispersion}
\end{equation}
where $g = 9.81$~m/s$^2$ and $d$ is the water depth.
This hard constraint reduces the parameter space, regularizes
optimization, and ensures physically plausible wave propagation.

Our key contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Grid-free spectral representation (C1)}: Direct estimation
    on sparse observations without grid interpolation, enabling arbitrary
    spatial resolution and scaling to any observation pattern.
  \item \textbf{Dispersion-constrained optimization (C2)}: Hard enforcement
    of the dispersion relation reduces parameters and acts as a physics
    regularizer, improving convergence on multi-component seas.
  \item \textbf{Fresnel-aware confidence weighting (C3)}: Per-observation
    weights based on incidence angle and range account for heteroscedastic
    LiDAR noise, critical for sloped sea surfaces.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive model complexity}: Energy-based pruning and
    residual-triggered growth automatically match the number of spectral
    components to scene complexity, from $N$=1 for simple swells to
    $N$=21 for complex multi-component seas.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Grid-Based Wave Field Estimation}
Desmars et al.~\cite{desmars2020} introduced ETKF-based wave field
reconstruction from X-band radar, with spectral propagation on a regular
grid. Huhn et al.~\cite{huhn2022} extended this to LiDAR observations,
demonstrating the ``from points to waves'' pipeline.
These methods discretize the 2D spatial domain (typically 40$\times$30 to
80$\times$60 grid points) and use FFT-based spectral propagation as the
forecast model.
Our approach eliminates the grid entirely, operating directly on spectral
components.

\subsection{Spectral Estimation Methods}
Classical spectral estimation (FFT, MUSIC, ESPRIT) requires regularly
sampled data and struggles with the irregular, sparse sampling inherent
to scanning LiDAR. Capon beamforming~\cite{capon1969} can handle irregular
arrays but assumes stationary signals.
WaveSplat combines beamforming for initialization with gradient-based
optimization for refinement, exploiting temporal structure through the
dispersion relation.

\subsection{Neural and Learning-Based Approaches}
Recent work has applied neural networks to wave prediction~\cite{zhang2022},
but these require large training datasets and generalize poorly across sea
states. WaveSplat is purely physics-based, with no training data required
and interpretable spectral parameters.

\subsection{Gaussian Splatting}
3D Gaussian Splatting~\cite{kerbl2023} revolutionized novel-view synthesis
by representing scenes as collections of anisotropic Gaussians optimized
via gradient descent.
WaveSplat adapts this philosophy to wave fields: each ``Gaussian'' is a
plane wave component in spectral space, optimized to fit sparse LiDAR
observations while respecting physical constraints.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Forward Model}

The wave field is modeled as a superposition of $N$ linear plane
waves~(\ref{eq:forward}).
Each component $i$ has four free parameters: amplitude $w_i$,
wavenumber components $(k_{x,i}, k_{y,i})$, and phase offset
$\varphi_i$. The angular frequency $\omega_i$ is deterministically
computed from the dispersion relation~(\ref{eq:dispersion}),
reducing the total parameter count from $5N$ to $4N$.

This linear model is an $O(\epsilon^2)$ approximation to the Gerstner
wave model used in our ground-truth simulator, where
$\epsilon = |k| \cdot |w|$ is the wave steepness. At Beaufort~4,
$\epsilon \approx 0.05$, giving model mismatch $< 0.25\%$.

\subsection{Observation Model}

At each LiDAR scan at time $t$, we observe $M$ elevation measurements
$\{(x_j, y_j, z_j)\}_{j=1}^{M}$ at irregular spatial locations.
The observation model is:
\begin{equation}
  z_j = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cos(k_{x,i} x_j + k_{y,i} y_j - \omega_i t + \varphi_i) + \eta_j
\end{equation}
where $\eta_j$ is observation noise with variance depending on range
and incidence angle.

\subsection{Fresnel-Zone Confidence Weighting (C3)}

LiDAR elevation accuracy degrades with range (beam divergence) and
incidence angle (specular reflection probability). We assign per-observation
confidence weights:
\begin{equation}
  c_j = \frac{\cos(\theta_j)}{d_j / d_{\text{ref}}}
  \label{eq:confidence}
\end{equation}
where $\theta_j$ is the beam incidence angle, $d_j$ is the range,
and $d_{\text{ref}} = 125$~m is a reference distance. These weights
are incorporated in the optimization loss as weighted MSE.

\subsection{Two-Phase Estimation}

\subsubsection{Phase 1: Initialization}
Given the first $T_{\text{init}} = 2 T_p$ seconds of observations
(where $T_p$ is the expected peak period), we jointly optimize all
spectral parameters using gradient descent with a coarse-to-fine
schedule ($N$=3 $\to$ 8 $\to$ 15).

Initial values are obtained via \textbf{dispersion-aware beamforming}:
for each candidate wavenumber $(k_x, k_y)$, we compute the
corresponding $\omega$ from~(\ref{eq:dispersion}) and evaluate the
beamforming power:
\begin{equation}
  P(k_x, k_y) = \left| \sum_{j} z_j \, e^{-i(k_x x_j + k_y y_j - \omega t_j)} \right|^2
\end{equation}
Peaks in $P$ are extracted greedily with neighborhood suppression,
providing accurate initial estimates of $(k_x, k_y)$.
Amplitudes and phases are then estimated via linear projection.

The optimization minimizes:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_j c_j \, (z_j - \hat{z}_j)^2
    + \lambda_1 \sum_i |w_i|
    + \lambda_s \sum_i (w_{\sigma(i)} - w_{\sigma(i-1)})^2
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_1 = 10^{-3}$ promotes sparsity and $\lambda_s = 10^{-4}$
penalizes spectral roughness ($\sigma$ sorts by $|k|$).

\subsubsection{Adaptive N}
After initialization, we apply energy-based pruning: components are
sorted by energy $w_i^2$ in descending order, and the fewest components
capturing 90\% of total energy are retained. A safety check verifies
that pruning does not increase the initialization residual by more
than 30\%; if so, pruning is rolled back.

If the residual RMS after pruning exceeds a threshold (0.05~m), new
components are grown via beamforming on the residual signal, up to
$N_{\max}=30$.

\subsubsection{Phase 2: Tracking}
For each subsequent frame, we:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Predict}: advance phases by
    $\varphi_i \mathrel{+}= \omega_i \, \Delta t$
  \item \textbf{Update}: perform $K=10$ gradient steps on the current
    frame, updating only $(w_i, \varphi_i)$ while freezing wavenumbers
    (\texttt{w\_phi} mode)
\end{enumerate}

Freezing $(k_x, k_y)$ during tracking prevents overfitting to
single-frame noise and exploits the dispersion constraint: once
wavenumbers are accurately estimated, phase prediction via
$\omega \cdot \Delta t$ provides a strong warm start.

Multi-frame tracking uses a sliding window of $K_w=3$ recent frames
for gradient computation, improving stability without significant
computational overhead.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Simulation Environment}

We use a physics-based LiDAR wave simulation (WaveSim) generating
ground-truth Gerstner wave fields with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sea states}: Beaufort 2--5 ($H_s$=0.3--2.0~m, $T_p$=4--9~s)
  \item \textbf{Wave complexity}: 1, 2, 5, 15, 30 spectral components
  \item \textbf{LiDAR model}: 500 horizontal beams, 10~Hz scan rate
  \item \textbf{Raydrop models}: uniform (constant drop probability)
    and slope-dependent (higher dropout on steep surfaces)
  \item \textbf{Water depth}: $d = 50$~m
\end{itemize}

Each episode contains 301 frames (30~s at 10~Hz) with ground-truth
elevation fields on a 80$\times$60 evaluation grid.
A total of 100 episodes are generated across 5 random seeds per
configuration.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{nRMSE}: RMSE normalized by significant wave height $H_s$,
    the primary metric
  \item \textbf{Inference speed}: ms/frame for tracking phase
  \item \textbf{$N_{\text{adapt}}$}: final number of spectral components
    (adaptive $N$)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Baselines}

\textbf{ETKF-grid}~\cite{huhn2022}: Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter
on a 40$\times$30 regular grid with 50 ensemble members,
FFT-based spectral propagation, multiplicative ($\alpha$=1.01) and
additive ($\sigma$=0.005) inflation, and observation thinning to
$M_{\max}$=40 per frame.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Grid-free advantages.}
The most striking result is the 8.5$\times$ advantage on 2-component
seas (nRMSE 0.052 vs.\ 0.444). For low-complexity wave fields, ETKF
wastes capacity representing the full spatial grid when only two spectral
modes are present. WaveSplat's adaptive representation directly captures
the relevant modes.

\textbf{Limitations.}
For very high complexity ($N_{\text{true}} \geq 30$), WaveSplat's
beamforming initialization cannot resolve all components, leading to
nRMSE $\sim$0.30. A spatio-temporal 3D FFT or higher-resolution
beamforming could address this.
The inference speed of 13.4~ms is slower than P0 (8.4~ms) due to
multi-frame tracking; this could be mitigated by reducing the window
size or parallelizing gradient computation.

\textbf{Dispersion constraint.}
The ablation reveals a nuanced role for the dispersion constraint:
it acts more as a regularizer than a strict accuracy improvement.
On moderate seas, the optimizer can find better solutions without it,
but for tracking stability over long time horizons, the
$\omega = f(|k|, d)$ constraint prevents phase drift.

% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented WaveSplat, a grid-free spectral Gaussian approach to
ocean wave field estimation from sparse LiDAR.
By representing waves as optimizable spectral components with
dispersion constraints and adaptive complexity, WaveSplat achieves
51.5\% lower nRMSE than ETKF-grid while running 2.4$\times$ faster.
The method wins on all 12 test episodes spanning Beaufort 2--5.

Future work includes extending to real LiDAR data, incorporating
current estimation, and exploring learned initialization for
faster convergence on complex seas.
