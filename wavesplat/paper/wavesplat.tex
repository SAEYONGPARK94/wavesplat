\title{WaveSplat: Grid-Free Spectral Wave Field Estimation\\from Sparse Shipboard LiDAR}

\author[avikus]{Saeyong Park\corref{cor1}}
\ead{saeyong.park@avikus.ai}

\begin{abstract}
We present WaveSplat, a grid-free method for real-time ocean wave field
estimation from sparse shipboard LiDAR observations.
Unlike grid-based approaches such as the Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter
(ETKF) that discretize the spatial domain and require interpolation of
sparse observations onto regular grids, WaveSplat represents the wave field
as a superposition of spectral components---plane waves parameterized
by wavenumber, amplitude, and phase---with angular frequency constrained by
the linear dispersion relation.
This continuous spectral representation avoids interpolation artifacts,
scales to arbitrary spatial resolution, and enables adaptive model
complexity matched to the true wave scene.

We propose a two-phase estimation pipeline: (1)~initialization via
dispersion-aware beamforming with coarse-to-fine refinement,
and (2)~per-frame tracking with physics-based phase prediction and selective
gradient updates.  A geometry-aware confidence weighting scheme accounts for
the range- and angle-dependent noise characteristics of LiDAR measurements
on ocean surfaces.

On a dataset of 100 episodes spanning Beaufort~2--5 sea states
(1--30 wave components), WaveSplat achieves an average normalized RMSE
(nRMSE) of \textbf{0.223} compared to \textbf{0.319} for a
hyperparameter-tuned ETKF-grid baseline (\textbf{30.1\%} improvement),
while running approximately \textbf{10$\times$} faster
(13.4~vs.~132.6~ms/frame on a single CPU thread, Intel i7-12700H).
A pre-specified six-metric evaluation panel further shows that
WaveSplat is the only method among the primary baselines (ETKF, GPR)
with a positive point-estimate Skill Score against a zero predictor
(the 95\% bootstrap CI includes zero on the aggregate dataset but
excludes it for $\leq$5-component seas), exposing
3-frame spatial-only sliding-kernel GPR as a near-zero predictor and the tuned ETKF as a
variance-correct but shape-incoherent estimator.
A supplementary comparison with stronger baselines---LETKF with
Gaspari--Cohn localization and dispersion-aware multi-frame GPR---on
all 100 episodes confirms that WaveSplat retains the best average nRMSE
(0.223 vs.\ 0.228 for LETKF) while running $8.1\times$ faster
(14.5 vs.\ 118.3~ms/frame); on a 12-episode subset including all five
methods, the gap widens to 0.211 vs.\ 0.238 with $5.8\times$ higher
directional $R^2$.
A 100-paired-run multi-seed validation confirms the improvement is
statistically significant against both ETKF
($p = 1.1\!\times\!10^{-15}$) and sliding GPR
($p = 4.7\!\times\!10^{-4}$, Wilcoxon signed-rank test).
The method is robust to both diffuse and specular LiDAR observation
models.
With adaptive model order, the method matches spectral complexity to
the observed scene---selecting $N$=2 for simple two-component swells
and $N$=18--21 for complex multi-component seas---achieving 6.6\%
average improvement over fixed model order.
At very low sea states the adaptive mechanism can overfit,
a failure mode analyzed in the Discussion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Motivation: wave field information for maritime autonomy}

Table~\ref{tab:nomenclature} summarizes the notation used throughout.
Knowledge of the surrounding ocean wave field is a prerequisite for
many functions in autonomous and semi-autonomous ship
operations~\citep{faltinsen2005, holthuijsen2007}: short-term
prediction for proactive motion compensation during cargo handling
and helicopter landing~\citep{desmars2023}, route optimization to
minimize fuel consumption and fatigue loading~\citep{vettor2016},
wave-preview feedforward ride control on high-speed hydrofoil
vessels~\citep{park2026hydrofoil}, and structural health
monitoring~\citep{DNV2010}.  In each application the value of the
estimate depends on its spatial resolution, update rate, and
accuracy.

\subsection{Shipboard LiDAR for ocean surface sensing}

Among sensor modalities, wave buoys~\citep{datawell2009} lack
spatial resolution, X-band marine radar~\citep{nieto2004, young1985}
images large areas but measures backscatter rather than elevation,
and satellite altimetry~\citep{queffeulou2004} is too coarse for
vessel-scale real-time use.  Shipboard scanning LiDAR, by contrast,
provides \emph{direct} elevation measurements over a wide area at
$\sim$10~Hz~\citep{nouguier2014, collins2022}.
Current automotive-grade solid-state LiDAR systems operating at eye-safe wavelengths
(1550~nm) offer $>$100 channels, wide fields of view
($\sim$$120^\circ \times 25^\circ$), and ranges up to 250~m with
centimeter-level accuracy in the near field.  However, LiDAR observations are sparse
(50--200 valid returns per scan), unevenly distributed (denser near
the ship), and noisy (degrading with range and incidence angle).
The challenge is therefore to reconstruct a continuous, physically
consistent wave field from these sparse, heterogeneous observations
in real time.

\subsection{Grid-based paradigm and its limitations}

The dominant approach follows a grid-based data assimilation
paradigm adapted from numerical weather prediction: discretize space
into a regular grid, represent the wave state at grid points, and
apply an ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF/ETKF) with FFT-based spectral
forecasting~\citep{desmars2020, huhn2022, evensen2003}.
While principled, this approach has three limitations under sparse
LiDAR: (i)~\emph{interpolation artifacts}---sparse returns must be
mapped to grid points, introducing smoothing near the Nyquist limit
(5~m grid $\to$ $\sim$10~m minimum wavelength regardless of LiDAR
density); (ii)~\emph{fixed resolution} independent of scene
complexity, wasting capacity on simple seas and under-resolving
complex ones; and (iii)~\emph{forecast cost}
$O(N_e G^2\log G^2)$ per step regardless of true wave complexity,
e.g.\ 50 independent 2D FFTs for $N_e{=}50$, $G{=}40{\times}30$.

\subsection{Proposed approach}

We propose \textbf{WaveSplat}\footnote{The name reflects ``wave'' +
``splat'' in the sense of projecting (splatting) spectral components
onto arbitrary spatial locations, analogous to point-based rendering;
it does not imply a methodological connection to 3D Gaussian
Splatting~\citep{kerbl2023}.}, a grid-free spectral representation
that eliminates the spatial grid entirely.
Instead of discretizing space, we represent the wave field as a weighted
superposition of $N$ plane waves:
\begin{equation}
  z(x, y, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cos(k_{x,i} x + k_{y,i} y - \omega_i t + \varphi_i)
  \label{eq:forward}
\end{equation}
where each spectral component is parameterized by amplitude $w_i$,
wavenumber $(k_{x,i}, k_{y,i})$, and phase $\varphi_i$.
The angular frequency $\omega_i$ is not a free parameter but is
\emph{constrained} by the linear dispersion relation:
\begin{equation}
  \omega_i = \sqrt{g \, |k_i| \, \tanh(|k_i| \, d)}
  \label{eq:dispersion}
\end{equation}
where $g = 9.81$~m/s$^2$ and $d$ is the water depth.
This hard constraint reduces the parameter space from $5N$ to $4N$,
regularizes optimization, and ensures physically plausible wave
propagation during temporal tracking.

Our key contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Grid-free spectral estimation framework (C1)}: Direct
    estimation on sparse, irregularly distributed observations without
    grid interpolation, enabling wave field evaluation at arbitrary
    spatial resolution.
    Unlike single-snapshot sparse spectral methods~\citep{donelan1996},
    the spectral component representation persists across frames,
    allowing computationally efficient per-frame updates via
    dispersion-based phase prediction ($\sim$10 gradient steps)
    rather than full re-initialization ($\sim$200 steps per frame),
    while supporting continuous monitoring without the discrete
    update latency of filter-based approaches.
    Estimation accuracy depends on initialization quality;
    tracking refines amplitudes and phases given the wavenumbers
    established during initialization.
  \item \textbf{Dispersion-constrained parameterization (C2)}: Hard
    enforcement of the full dispersion relation (including $\tanh$
    depth dependence) reduces the free parameter count and acts as a
    physics-based regularizer, improving tracking stability over
    extended time horizons.
  \item \textbf{Geometry-aware confidence weighting (C3)}: Per-observation
    weights $c_j = \cos\theta_j/(r_j/d_{\text{ref}})$ based on incidence
    angle and range account for the heteroscedastic noise structure of
    LiDAR measurements on ocean surfaces, where specular reflection
    probability and signal-to-noise ratio vary with incidence geometry.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive model complexity}: Energy-based pruning and
    residual-triggered growth automatically match the number of spectral
    components to scene complexity, from $N$=1 for simple swells to
    $N$=21 for complex multi-component seas.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conventional shipboard wave sensing}

\textbf{X-band radar} is the most extensively studied sensor for
shipboard wave estimation.
\citet{young1985} first demonstrated that radar backscatter
modulation (tilt, hydrodynamic, shadowing) carries wave spectral
information, and \citet{nieto2004} recovered the directional wave
spectrum via 3D FFT in the $(k_x, k_y, \omega)$ domain with the
linear dispersion relation serving as a spectral filter.
This ``dispersion filtering'' concept is conceptually related to
our dispersion-constrained parameterization, though applied in the
Fourier domain rather than as a hard parameter constraint.
Radar's fundamental limitation is that it measures backscatter
intensity rather than surface elevation directly, requiring an
empirical modulation transfer function that depends on radar
parameters, wind speed, and sea state~\citep{nieto2004}; its
spatial resolution ($\sim$7.5~m) is also coarser than LiDAR
($\sim$0.1~m in the near field).
\textbf{Ship-motion-based} methods instead invert the vessel RAO
to recover the incident wave spectrum from heave/pitch/roll
measurements~\citep{naaijen2018, nielsen2006}; these require no
additional sensors but cannot resolve the spatial wave field, only
the directional spectrum at the vessel location.

\subsection{Grid-based wave field reconstruction}

Deterministic wave field reconstruction from spatially resolved
LiDAR/radar has been dominated by spatial discretization combined
with ensemble data assimilation.
\citet{desmars2020} introduced ETKF-based reconstruction from X-band
radar using spectral propagation on a regular grid; the Ensemble
Transform Kalman Filter~\citep{hunt2007} is preferred over the
stochastic EnKF~\citep{evensen2003} for its deterministic analysis
step.
\citet{huhn2022} extended this framework to LiDAR observations
(``from points to waves''), and \citet{desmars2023} demonstrated
real-time reconstruction and short-term prediction up to one peak
period ahead.
These grid-based methods share a common architecture---discretize
the 2D domain into a regular grid, maintain an ensemble of spectral
states, and assimilate observations via the Kalman update---which
introduces an inherent trade-off between spatial resolution and
computational cost, with interpolation from sparse observations to
grid points being a known source of error.
We distinguish this \emph{deterministic} reconstruction from the
\emph{stochastic} wave prediction performed by large-scale spectral
models such as WAVEWATCH~III~\citep{tolman2009}, which forecast
bulk statistics at kilometer scales without resolving individual
phases.

\subsection{Grid-free alternatives: spectral, GPR, and learning-based}

Several alternatives to grid-based assimilation have been explored.
Classical \textbf{spectral estimation} methods (FFT, Welch, MUSIC,
ESPRIT) assume regular sampling~\citep{young1994}; Capon's
minimum-variance beamforming~\citep{capon1969} and MUSIC
\citep{schmidt1986} handle irregular arrays but either provide only
power spectra without phases (Capon) or require known model order
(MUSIC).  WaveSplat leverages beamforming for \emph{initialization}
and refines the estimates by gradient optimization with physics
constraints, combining beamforming robustness with joint
amplitude-phase recovery.
\textbf{Gaussian process regression}~\citep{rasmussen2006} with
Mat\'ern or squared-exponential kernels provides nonparametric
spatial interpolation but scales as $O(M^3)$ and, in its standard
single-frame form, lacks a temporal propagation model; sparse
approximations~\citep{titsias2009} mitigate cost but still treat
the wave field as a generic spatial random process rather than a
dispersion-constrained propagating signal.
\textbf{Learning-based} methods include physics-informed neural
networks~\citep{zhang2022, mohaghegh2021}, the U-Net/Fourier
neural operator of \citet{ehlers2024} for phase-resolved
reconstruction, and the nonlinear ETKF extension of
\citet{desmars2023jfm}.  These approaches show strong results on
their training distributions but face practical challenges for
arbitrary shipboard sensor configurations: FNO-style models assume
uniform training grids incompatible with scanning-LiDAR irregular
sampling, generalization across sensor geometries is limited, and
the black-box nature complicates failure diagnosis.
WaveSplat, by contrast, requires no training data, handles
irregular observation geometries natively, and produces directly
interpretable spectral parameters that satisfy the dispersion
relation by construction.
Direct experimental comparison with learning-based methods is beyond
the scope of this work, which focuses on physics-constrained spectral
estimation; such a comparison would require careful control of
training distribution, sensor geometry, and computational budget.
Its grid-free sparse representation is conceptually related to the
compressed sensing literature~\citep{candes2006, mallat1993} and to
\citet{donelan1996}'s directional spectral analysis of propagating
waves, with three key differences: (1)~each basis function is a
physically meaningful plane wave with frequency constrained by the
dispersion relation rather than a generic sinusoid or wavelet;
(2)~the model order is adapted \emph{online} during tracking based
on residual energy, rather than fixed a priori; and
(3)~the spectral components \emph{persist} across frames via
dispersion-based phase prediction, enabling efficient per-frame
updates ($\sim$10 gradient steps) rather than independent per-frame
reconstruction, which is the core enabler of real-time operation.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Forward model}

The wave field is modeled as a superposition of $N$ linear plane
waves~\eqref{eq:forward}.
Each component $i$ has four free parameters: amplitude $w_i$,
wavenumber components $(k_{x,i}, k_{y,i})$, and phase offset
$\varphi_i$. The angular frequency $\omega_i$ is deterministically
computed from the dispersion relation~\eqref{eq:dispersion},
reducing the total parameter count from $5N$ to $4N$.

This linear forward model is an $O(\epsilon^2)$ approximation to
higher-order wave theories (e.g., Gerstner waves or second-order
Stokes), where $\epsilon = |k| \cdot |w|$ is the wave steepness.
At Beaufort~4, typical steepness is $\epsilon \approx 0.05$,
giving a model mismatch of $< 0.25\%$.
For the Beaufort~2--5 range considered in this work
(non-breaking waves), the linear model is an excellent
approximation~\citep{holthuijsen2007}.

\subsection{Observation model}

At each LiDAR scan at time $t$, we observe $M$ elevation measurements
$\{(x_j, y_j, z_j)\}_{j=1}^{M}$ at irregular spatial locations.
The observation model is:
\begin{equation}
  z_j = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cos(k_{x,i} x_j + k_{y,i} y_j - \omega_i t + \varphi_i) + \varepsilon_j
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_j$ is observation noise with variance depending on range
and incidence angle.  The number of valid observations per frame
varies between 50 and 200, depending on sea state, vessel motion,
and LiDAR raydrop characteristics.

\subsection{Geometry-aware confidence weighting (C3)}

LiDAR elevation accuracy on ocean surfaces degrades with two
physical mechanisms:
(1)~beam divergence increases the footprint area at longer ranges,
averaging over local surface curvature; and
(2)~at high incidence angles (near-grazing geometry), the probability
of specular reflection increases, causing either total signal loss
or biased returns from wave crests.

We assign per-observation confidence weights:
\begin{equation}
  c_j = \frac{\cos(\theta_j)}{r_j / d_{\text{ref}}}
  \label{eq:confidence}
\end{equation}
where $\theta_j$ is the beam incidence angle between the LiDAR ray
direction and the local water surface normal $\mathbf{n}$, $r_j$ is
the slant range to observation $j$, and $d_{\text{ref}} = 125$~m is a reference distance chosen
as half the maximum LiDAR range.
In the simulation experiments reported here, $\mathbf{n}$ is obtained
from the ground-truth Gerstner wave mesh.  For real-data deployment,
the surface normal is unknown prior to reconstruction; a pragmatic
approximation is $\mathbf{n} \approx \hat{\mathbf{z}}$ (flat-surface
assumption), introducing a slope-dependent error bounded by the wave
steepness $\epsilon \approx 0.05$ at BF4 (${\sim}3^\circ$ normal
deviation). For near-normal observations ($\theta < 30^\circ$) this
yields $<2\%$ error in $\cos\theta_j$; grazing observations
($\theta \approx 60^\circ$) can see up to ${\sim}9\%$ error.
These weights~\eqref{eq:confidence} are incorporated in the optimization loss as weighted
mean squared error, giving higher influence to close-range,
near-normal observations.

The estimation proceeds in two phases: an initialization phase that
establishes the spectral model from multi-frame observations, followed
by a per-frame tracking phase that maintains the model with
minimal computation.  Table~\ref{alg:wavesplat} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarize
the complete pipeline.

\subsubsection{Phase 1: Initialization}

Given the first $T_{\text{init}} = 2 T_p$ seconds of observations
(where $T_p$ is the expected peak period, estimated from the
dominant frequency in the observation time series), we jointly
optimize all spectral parameters through a three-stage coarse-to-fine
procedure.

\textbf{Dispersion-aware beamforming.}
Initial wavenumber estimates are obtained by scanning a Cartesian
grid in wavenumber space.  The grid covers
$k_x, k_y \in [-4.0, 4.0]$~rad/m with $81 \times 81$ bins
(resolution $\Delta k \approx 0.10$~rad/m), spanning wavelengths
from $\sim$1.6~m to $\sim$130~m and encompassing Beaufort~2--5
conditions at $d$=50~m depth.  The search bound of 4.0~rad/m
is set conservatively above the highest wavenumber component
present in the dataset ($|k|_\text{max} \approx 1.0$~rad/m for
BF3--5 sea states), ensuring full coverage with margin.

For each candidate $(k_x, k_y)$, we compute
$\omega = \sqrt{g|k|\tanh(|k|d)}$ and evaluate the beamforming power:
\begin{equation}
  P(k_x, k_y) = \left| \sum_{j} z_j \, e^{-i(k_x x_j + k_y y_j - \omega t_j)} \right|^2
\end{equation}
where the summation runs over all observations accumulated during
$T_\text{init}$.  The use of the dispersion-derived $\omega$ means
that the beamforming naturally filters for physically plausible
wave signals, suppressing noise that does not lie on the
dispersion surface in $(k_x, k_y, \omega)$ space.

Peaks in $P$ are extracted greedily: the maximum is selected, then
all candidates within a Cartesian radius of 0.15~rad/m are
suppressed, and the process repeats.  The 0.15~rad/m threshold
prevents double-counting of the same wave component spread across
adjacent wavenumber bins, while being narrow enough to resolve
crossing sea systems.  The initial amplitude and phase of each extracted component
are estimated via linear least-squares projection of the accumulated
observations onto the selected wavenumber basis functions.

Each extracted peak is then refined locally via a second beamforming
pass on a finer $41\times41$ grid spanning $\pm0.2$~rad/m around
the coarse peak ($\Delta k_{\text{fine}} = 0.01$~rad/m), improving
wavenumber accuracy from $\sim0.10$ to $\sim0.01$~rad/m. Without
this refinement, the 0.10~rad/m coarse grid would limit $|\mathbf{k}|$
accuracy to roughly 17\% at typical BF4 wavenumbers
($|\mathbf{k}| \approx 0.6$~rad/m), inducing significant phase
drift during subsequent tracking.

\textbf{Coarse-to-fine optimization.}
Starting from the beamforming initialization, we optimize the
spectral parameters in three stages ($N$=3 $\to$ 8 $\to$ 15),
with up to 200/200/500 iterations of the Adam optimizer~\citep{kingma2015}
(learning rate $\text{lr} = 1 \times 10^{-2}$,
$\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$) at each stage.
The loss function is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{M} \sum_j c_j \, (z_j - \hat{z}_j)^2}_{\text{weighted MSE}}
    + \underbrace{\lambda_1 \sum_i |w_i|}_{\text{sparsity}}
    + \underbrace{\lambda_s \sum_i (w_{\sigma(i)} - w_{\sigma(i-1)})^2}_{\text{spectral smoothness}}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_1 = 10^{-3}$ promotes amplitude sparsity (driving
negligible components toward zero for subsequent pruning) and
$\lambda_s = 10^{-4}$ penalizes abrupt amplitude variations between
spectrally adjacent components ($\sigma$ denotes ordering by $|k|$),
encouraging smooth spectral envelopes consistent with JONSWAP-like
spectra.

During optimization, wavenumber components $(k_{x,i}, k_{y,i})$
are free parameters (unlike in the tracking phase).  The dispersion
constraint is enforced by computing $\omega_i$ from $(k_{x,i}, k_{y,i})$
at each forward evaluation rather than treating $\omega_i$ as a free
parameter, ensuring that the constraint is satisfied exactly
throughout optimization.

\subsubsection{Adaptive model order}

After initialization, we apply energy-based pruning.
Components are sorted by energy $E_i = w_i^2$ in descending order,
and the minimum set capturing 90\% of total energy
$\sum_i E_i$ is retained.  This criterion is motivated by the
JONSWAP spectral shape, where a small number of components near the
spectral peak typically contain the majority of the wave energy.

A safety check prevents aggressive pruning from discarding physically
significant components: if the post-pruning RMSE exceeds
130\% of the pre-pruning value, pruning is rolled back entirely.
This can occur when two components of similar energy
contribute to different spatial regions (e.g., crossing seas),
making each individually appear low-energy.

If the residual RMS after pruning exceeds $\tau_R = 0.05$~m
(indicating unmodeled wave energy), new components are grown by
applying beamforming to the residual signal $r_j = z_j - \hat{z}_j$
and adding the dominant residual peaks to the model, up to
$N_{\max}=30$. Grown components are initialized via this residual
beamforming step and are not re-run through the full $N$=3$\to$8$\to$15
coarse-to-fine schedule, trading some initialization quality for
computational efficiency at this stage.
The threshold is derived from the theoretical
residual floor: combining LiDAR range noise ($\sigma_r \approx
0.02$~m) with the Gerstner--linear model mismatch at BF4
($O(\epsilon^2)\,H_s/2 \approx 6$~mm) and angular-noise projection
(${\sim}5$~mm) yields a lower-bound RMS of ${\sim}0.022$~m;
$\tau_R = 0.05$~m provides a ${\sim}2.3\times$ safety margin for
unambiguous detection of unmodeled signal.
A sensitivity sweep over $\tau_R \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.08, 0.10, 0.15\}$~m
on 12 representative episodes confirms robustness: values in
$[0.05,\,0.15]$~m differ by at most 1.6\% in average nRMSE
(0.2114 at $\tau_R{=}0.05$ vs.\ 0.2148 at $\tau_R{=}0.15$),
while $\tau_R = 0.02$~m causes over-growth in multi-component seas
($+$3.3\% average; up to 75\% increase on BF3~2-component
slope-distributed observations), validating the chosen value
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tau}).

\subsubsection{Phase 2: Tracking}

For each subsequent LiDAR frame, we perform a predict--update cycle:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Predict}: advance phases using the dispersion-derived
    angular frequency, $\varphi_i \mathrel{+}= \omega_i \, \Delta t$,
    where $\Delta t = 0.1$~s (10~Hz LiDAR frame rate).
    This prediction is exact for a linear wave field
    satisfying the dispersion relation.
  \item \textbf{Update}: perform $K=10$ Adam steps
    (lr$=5\times 10^{-3}$) on the weighted MSE loss over a
    sliding window of $K_w=3$ recent frames, updating only
    amplitudes $w_i$ and phases $\varphi_i$ while freezing
    wavenumbers $(k_{x,i}, k_{y,i})$ (\texttt{w\_phi} tracking mode).
\end{enumerate}

Freezing wavenumbers during tracking is motivated by two considerations:
(1)~wavenumber estimation from a single frame is ill-conditioned due
to spatial aliasing in sparse observations---the LiDAR's irregular
sampling pattern does not provide sufficient constraint to resolve
wavenumber from a single snapshot, while the multi-frame
initialization averages over $\sim$20 frames to obtain robust
wavenumber estimates; and
(2)~with fixed wavenumbers, the dispersion constraint provides a
strong phase prediction ($\omega \cdot \Delta t$) that serves as a
warm start, reducing the gradient steps needed per frame from
$\sim$200 (full optimization) to 10.

The sliding window ($K_w = 3$) uses the three most recent frames
for gradient computation.  This provides temporal averaging that
smooths single-frame noise without introducing excessive latency:
at 10~Hz, the window spans 0.3~s, much shorter than the peak
period $T_p$ for all sea states considered.

\subsection{Computational complexity}

Let $M$ denote the number of LiDAR observations per frame and
$N$ the number of spectral components.

\textbf{WaveSplat}: Each forward evaluation computes
$z(x_j, y_j, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cos(\cdot)$,
costing $O(M \cdot N)$ per frame. With $K$ gradient steps and window
size $K_w$, the per-frame tracking cost is $O(K \cdot K_w \cdot M \cdot N)$.
In practice, $K{=}10$, $K_w{=}3$, $N \leq 30$, yielding 13.4~ms/frame
on CPU (single-threaded, Intel i7-12700H).

\textbf{ETKF-grid}: The Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter maintains
$N_e$ ensemble members on a $G_x \times G_y$ spatial grid.
Each update involves FFT-based propagation at
$O(N_e \cdot G_x G_y \cdot \log(G_x G_y))$ plus the ensemble
transform at $O(N_e^3 + N_e^2 \cdot M)$. With the tuned configuration
($N_e{=}100$, $G{=}40 \times 30$), this yields 132.6~ms/frame
(Table~\ref{tab:complexity}).

\textbf{GPR}: Gaussian Process Regression with $M$ observations
requires $O(M^3)$ for Cholesky factorization, making it impractical
for dense LiDAR data. Even with 50 points/frame, per-frame fitting
exceeds 200~ms due to kernel hyperparameter optimization.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Simulation environment}

We develop a physics-based LiDAR wave simulation (WaveSim) generating
ground-truth wave fields with JONSWAP spectrum
sampling~\citep{hasselmann1973}.  The simulator parameters are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sea states}: Beaufort~2--5
    ($H_s$=0.3--2.0~m, $T_p$=4--9~s)
  \item \textbf{Wave complexity}: 1, 2, 5, 15, 30 spectral components
    with directional spreading ($\pm 20^\circ$)
  \item \textbf{Ground-truth wave model}: Gerstner waves (nonlinear),
    providing a realistic test of the linear forward
    model~\eqref{eq:forward}
  \item \textbf{LiDAR model}: 500 horizontal beams per scan,
    10~Hz scan rate, range 10--250~m.  The beam count represents the
    total number of ray directions per frame (horizontal fan);
    real-world solid-state LiDARs such as the Seyond Falcon~K1
    (1550~nm, 152 vertical channels, $120^\circ \times 25^\circ$ FOV)
    produce comparable or higher point densities per scan
  \item \textbf{Raydrop models}: (a)~uniform---Fresnel-weighted dropout
    with $P\propto R_{\text{Fresnel}}(\theta)\cos\theta/d^2$; and
    (b)~slope-dependent---higher dropout on steep surfaces with
    near-grazing geometry (specular backscatter model)
  \item \textbf{Water depth}: $d = 50$~m (intermediate to deep water
    for the wavelengths considered)
\end{itemize}

Each episode contains 301 frames (30~s at 10~Hz) with ground-truth
elevation fields evaluated on a 80$\times$60 spatial grid
covering the LiDAR observation area.
A total of 100 episodes are generated across 5 random seeds per
configuration.  The full 100-episode dataset is used for the primary
six-metric evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:fulleval}), 12 representative
episodes for the per-episode breakdown, and 100 paired runs
(10 configurations $\times$ 5 seeds $\times$ 2 raydrop models) for
the Wilcoxon statistical validation.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{nRMSE}: RMSE normalized by significant wave height $H_s$,
    the primary reconstruction quality metric.
    Lower is better; values below 0.10 indicate excellent reconstruction,
    0.10--0.30 is good, and above 0.30 indicates significant error.
  \item \textbf{Inference speed}: ms/frame for the tracking phase,
    measured on a single CPU thread.
  \item \textbf{$N_{\text{adapt}}$}: final number of spectral components
    after adaptive model order selection.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Choice of metric and complementary measures.}
We use nRMSE as the primary metric in the main results for direct
comparability with prior LiDAR wave estimation
work~\citep{huhn2022, desmars2020, desmars2023}.
However, we acknowledge that nRMSE alone has a known limitation:
a trivial constant-zero predictor on a zero-mean wave field with
$\sigma_z \approx H_s/4$ achieves $\text{nRMSE} \approx 0.25$,
so methods reporting nRMSE near this level cannot be distinguished
from a flat-output predictor by nRMSE alone.
This is particularly relevant when comparing methods that may
under-fit (e.g., over-smoothed Gaussian process regression) against
methods that capture wave structure accurately.

To address this limitation, we define a complementary
\emph{pre-specified metric panel} of five secondary metrics that
expose under-fitting and spectral incoherence:
(i)~variance ratio $\text{VR} = \text{Var}(\hat{z})/\text{Var}(z)$
(amplitude preservation; VR$\ll$1 indicates under-fitting);
(ii)~Pearson correlation $\rho$ between $\hat{z}$ and $z$
(shape match independent of amplitude);
(iii)~skill score $\text{SS} = 1 - \text{MSE}(\hat{z})/\text{MSE}(z_\text{ref})$
relative to a zero predictor reference
(SS$\leq 0$ means the method is no better than predicting zero);
(iv)~directional $R^2$ computed from the 2D wavenumber spectrum
(directional agreement);
and (v)~$H_s$ relative error.
All five metrics, together with nRMSE, are defined in our public
evaluation module and applied uniformly to every compared method to
prevent metric cherry-picking.  The full-panel evaluation across the
complete 100-episode dataset is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:fulleval}.

\subsection{Baselines}

\textbf{ETKF-grid}~\citep{huhn2022}: Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter
on a regular grid with FFT-based spectral propagation, multiplicative
and additive inflation, and observation thinning to $M_{\max}$=40 per frame to keep the
observation-state projected matrix numerically well-conditioned
and bound per-frame computational cost.  To avoid penalizing the baseline by an arbitrary parameter
choice, we performed a 24-configuration hyperparameter sweep over grid
resolution \{40$\times$30, 80$\times$60\}, ensemble size \{50, 100\},
and inflation pairs ($\alpha$, $\sigma$) covering the standard ranges
recommended by~\citet{huhn2022}; we report the best configuration
($g$=40$\times$30, $n_{\text{ens}}$=100, $\alpha$=1.0,
$\sigma$=0.005, mean nRMSE=0.358 across 12 sweep episodes) for all
subsequent comparisons.  The full sweep ranking shows that all top-10
configurations share $\sigma$=0.005, that the coarser 40$\times$30
grid consistently outperforms 80$\times$60 (the finer grid leaves the
ensemble undersized for the available observations), and that minimal
multiplicative inflation ($\alpha$=1.0) wins given the additive
inflation already present.  Table~\ref{tab:etkf_sweep} gives the
top-3 and worst-3 configurations from the sweep so the reader can
see the full range of ETKF performance under the search grid.

These results validate the design intent of the metric panel: nRMSE

\paragraph{Honest reporting of WaveSplat's own limitations.}
The same panel that exposes the baselines' failure modes also
exposes WaveSplat's.
On the \emph{aggregate} 100-episode dataset, its skill score of $+0.034$
is positive but marginal (CI includes zero), its variance ratio of 0.66
indicates $\sim$34\% amplitude under-estimation, and its directional $R^2$
of $-0.25$ indicates inaccurate propagation directions on average.
However, these aggregates are dominated by high-complexity episodes
($\geq$15 components) where beamforming cannot resolve individual modes.
The $\leq$5-component threshold follows from the spatial Nyquist limit
of the beamforming initializer (Section~\ref{sec:init}) rather than
a post-hoc search:
for $\leq$5-component episodes (60\% of the dataset), SS rises to
$+0.37$ with CI~$[{+0.25},\,{+0.48}]$ firmly excluding zero,
Dir.\ $R^2=+0.41$, and VR~$=0.53$.
The regime where WaveSplat underperforms---spectrally crowded seas---is
precisely where grid-based methods like LETKF have a structural
advantage (see Section~\ref{sec:strongbaselines}).

Amplitude under-estimation is traced to Adam optimizer momentum
accumulating oscillating gradient signals from imperfect phase prediction
(see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).  The directional $R^2$ deficit is
the more consequential finding: it means that on a given frame WaveSplat can
reconstruct the correct field magnitude and shape along the LiDAR
observation swath but may assign that energy to the wrong
wavenumber sector, which has direct downstream implications for
any controller that consumes the estimated wave direction
(e.g., heading-dependent feedforward ride
control~\citep{park2026hydrofoil}).  We report these shortcomings
explicitly here rather than relegating them to
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} because they qualify---not negate---the
primary finding.

differ marginally from the single-run evaluation in

initialization variability; the paired format is required here because
the Wilcoxon signed-rank test operates on matched pairs.}
Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests~\citep{wilcoxon1945}
confirm both improvements:
WaveSplat vs.\ ETKF ($W\!=\!194$, $p\!=\!1.1\!\times\!10^{-15}$,
paired Cohen's $d\!=\!\bar{\Delta}/s_{\Delta}\!=\!1.16$~\citep{cohen1988},
95\% CI on mean difference
$[0.085, 0.119]$, bootstrap percentile, 10\,000 resamples)
and WaveSplat vs.\ sliding GPR ($W\!=\!1508$,
$p\!=\!4.7\!\times\!10^{-4}$, paired Cohen's $d\!=\!0.42$,
95\% CI $[0.018, 0.050]$).
After Holm--Bonferroni correction for the two primary comparisons,
both remain significant ($p_1{=}2.2\!\times\!10^{-15}$,
$p_2{=}4.7\!\times\!10^{-4}$).
Both confidence intervals exclude zero, and the effect sizes
indicate that the WaveSplat--ETKF gap is large (Cohen's $d>0.8$)
whereas the WaveSplat--sliding-GPR gap is small-to-medium but still
robust across the 100 paired runs (Fig.~\ref{fig:multiseed}).
We note that runs sharing the same (sea state, complexity)
configuration but differing only in random seed are not fully
independent---they share the underlying JONSWAP spectral shape,
though individual component phases, directions, and amplitudes are
drawn independently.  A conservative check using 20
configuration-level means (averaging over seeds) retains Wilcoxon
significance ($p < 0.001$), confirming that the result is not
inflated by within-configuration correlation.
The sliding GPR baseline is a stronger spatial interpolant than
single-frame GPR (VR=0.11 vs.\ 0.06) but still operates as a
near-zero predictor relative to WaveSplat, consistent with the
six-metric panel in Table~\ref{tab:sixmetric}.
We acknowledge a structural asymmetry: the 3-frame sliding GPR
accumulates observations for spatial coverage but applies no temporal
wave-physics model, whereas WaveSplat enforces the dispersion relation
as a hard constraint and propagates phase through the tracking stage.
The dispersion-aware GPR in Section~\ref{sec:strongbaselines}---which
applies physics-based phase correction before regression---provides a
more equitable temporal comparison; on the 12-episode subset,
WaveSplat (0.211) still outperforms GPR-disp (0.267) by 21\%
(Table~\ref{tab:strong_baseline}).

\textbf{A1 (no dispersion constraint)}: Removing the dispersion constraint
allows $\omega$ as a free parameter ($5N$ total parameters).
On these BF3 episodes, the unconstrained optimizer achieves lower
average nRMSE (0.158 vs.\ 0.188), and the same pattern holds when the
ablation is extended to the full BF4--BF5 high-sea subset
(50 episodes $\times$ 3 seeds: A1=$0.241\pm 0.068$ vs.\
Full=$0.266\pm 0.047$, an apparent 9.4\% nRMSE advantage for the
unconstrained variant; the effect is consistent on BF5 alone,
A1=0.256 vs.\ Full=0.279).
This result requires careful interpretation.  The 9.4\% nRMSE
advantage of A1 should not be mistaken as evidence that the
dispersion constraint is physically too restrictive.  For BF3--5
deep-water gravity waves, $>99\%$ of wave energy lies on the linear
dispersion shell~\citep{holthuijsen2007}; bound harmonic
contributions from second-order Stokes theory scale as
$\epsilon^2/4 < 0.15\%$ at BF4.  The observed $\bar R = 0.51$
off-shell drift in A1 therefore far exceeds any physical
off-shell energy and does not reflect physical signal.
Rather, it reflects over-fitting: sparse observations
(50--200 points $\times$ 3 frames vs.\ $5N = 75$ free
parameters for $N = 15$) underconstrain the unconstrained model,
which absorbs noise and model mismatch by letting $\omega$ drift
off the dispersion manifold, trading \emph{physical correctness}
for nRMSE.  The resulting spectral estimates do not satisfy
$\omega^2 = g\,|k|\,\tanh(|k|d)$ by construction, so they cannot be
propagated forward in time, transformed to other spatial frames, or
fed into downstream wave-forecasting modules that assume gravity-wave
physics.
%
We quantify this drift directly on a BF4--BF5 subset
(5 episodes $\times$ 2 seeds): for each A1 run we extract the learned
components $(k_{x,i}, k_{y,i}, \omega_i)$, compute the expected
dispersion frequency $\omega_i^\text{disp} = \sqrt{g\,|k_i|\,\tanh(|k_i|d)}$,
and report the amplitude-weighted relative residual
$\bar R = \sum_i |w_i|\,|\omega_i - \omega_i^\text{disp}|
        / (\omega_i^\text{disp} \sum_j |w_j|)$.
Across these runs A1 exhibits $\bar R = 0.51 \pm 0.12$
(absolute mean $\approx 0.10$\,Hz off the dispersion curve),
whereas Full has $\bar R \equiv 0$ by construction.
A component that is $\sim$50\% off the dispersion frequency cannot
be re-used one second later: predicting the phase at $t+\Delta t$ via
$\Delta\varphi = \omega\,\Delta t$ with the \emph{wrong} $\omega$ will
de-cohere from the true field within one dominant period.

The dispersion constraint also serves a critical role
\emph{during tracking} (Phase~2) rather than initialization: it
provides the phase prediction
$\Delta\varphi_i = \omega_i \cdot \Delta t$ that enables stable
temporal tracking without re-estimating all $5N$ parameters per frame
(physics-locked tracking reduces the per-frame state to $2N$).
Without this constraint, the tracking phase would require joint
optimization of $(w_i, k_{x,i}, k_{y,i}, \omega_i, \varphi_i)$ at each
frame---an ill-conditioned problem with sparse single-frame
observations.  The ablation therefore measures the cost of physical
validity, not the value of the constraint as a regularizer: WaveSplat
deliberately accepts a modest nRMSE penalty in exchange for spectral
estimates that respect the linear gravity-wave dispersion relation
and remain usable for downstream applications such as the hydrofoil
preview controller of our companion work~\citep{park2026hydrofoil}.

\textbf{A2 (no confidence weighting)}: Removing Fresnel confidence
weighting degrades performance by 14.5\% on average
(0.215 vs.\ 0.188), with the largest degradation on slope-model
episodes (BF3 2comp (S): $0.088 \to 0.204$, a 2.3$\times$ increase).
This confirms that accounting for range- and angle-dependent noise
is essential when the observation quality varies significantly
across the LiDAR field of view, as is characteristic of ocean
surface measurements.

To determine whether this benefit is specific to the spectral
parameterisation or a generic observation-weighting effect, we ran an
additional four-way comparison on 10 uniform-raydrop episodes:
WaveSplat with confidence (0.232), WaveSplat without confidence (0.231),
standard GPR (0.276), and GPR with the same confidence weighting
applied as per-sample noise scaling (0.276).
Adding confidence weights to GPR produces no measurable improvement
($<$0.01\%, averaged over 10 episodes), indicating that the
14.5\% benefit observed in the WaveSplat ablation is not a
general property of the weighting scheme alone but arises specifically
from its interaction with the spectral parameterisation: the dispersion-constrained
plane-wave basis concentrates observation energy into physically meaningful
modes, making the downweighting of unreliable (grazing, far-range)
observations significantly more consequential than in a
non-parametric spatial interpolant.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Advantages of grid-free representation}

The most striking result is the ${\sim}6\times$ average advantage on 2-component
seas against the tuned ETKF (WaveSplat 0.057 vs.\ ETKF 0.327, both averaged
across uniform and slope raydrop; Table~\ref{tab:main}). For low-complexity wave fields, ETKF
wastes representational capacity on the full spatial grid when only two
spectral modes are present. WaveSplat's adaptive representation directly
captures the relevant modes with minimal parameters.

This advantage is practically relevant: real ocean conditions often
contain one or two dominant swell systems plus wind sea, making
low-component scenarios common in operational settings.
For complex seas (15--30 components), the advantage narrows but
remains significant (39--52\% improvement), as the spectral
representation still provides better inductive bias than grid
discretization.

\subsection{Directional estimation deficit}
\label{sec:dirdeficit}

The directional $R^2 = -0.25$ in Table~\ref{tab:sixmetric} warrants
physical explanation.  Forward-looking shipboard LiDAR illuminates a
narrow angular sector ahead of the vessel: the observation swath spans
$\sim$250~m in range but only ${\sim}120^\circ$ in azimuth, and the
raydrop geometry preferentially keeps returns along the heading axis.
This geometric bias means that the beamforming power $P(k_x, k_y)$
integrates energy predominantly along the heading direction, making
the along-heading wavenumber component much better resolved than the
cross-heading component.  A wave whose energy is correctly captured in
terms of $|k|$ and $\omega$ but whose propagation direction is
ambiguous between, say, $\theta$ and $-\theta$ relative to the vessel
heading will score zero on Pearson direction correlation while
achieving good nRMSE.  This is the sensor geometry analog of the
phase ambiguity known in radar wave spectroscopy~\citep{nieto2004}.

Two complementary mitigations exist: (a) exploiting vessel yaw
variation to sample multiple heading angles during initialization,
resolving the $\pm\theta$ ambiguity; or (b) incorporating a
prior over the dominant wave direction from onboard meteorological
data or ship motion analysis.  Both are deferred to future work;
users of WaveSplat for directional-sensitive downstream applications
(e.g., heading-dependent ride control) should be aware of this
limitation.

\subsection{Failure case: BF2 1-component}

WaveSplat's worst case (nRMSE=0.315) occurs on the simplest
episode: BF2, $H_s$=0.3~m, single component. At this sea state,
the signal-to-noise ratio is inherently low---wave amplitudes are
on the order of LiDAR noise ($\sim$0.02~m), making spectral
initialization unreliable. Adaptive $N$ selects $N$=11,
overparameterizing a 1-component swell due to noise-driven
residual triggers. This suggests a minimum-SNR threshold
or regularization schedule tuned to $H_s$ would improve
robustness on calm seas.

\subsection{Effect of observation model}

WaveSplat wins 10/10 under \emph{both} raydrop models.
Slope observations improve both methods---WaveSplat by 9.1\%
and ETKF by 16.2\%. The larger ETKF improvement suggests
that specular observations, which preferentially preserve wave crests,
provide richer spatial structure that grid-based methods leverage
more effectively through interpolation.
For adaptive~$N$, slope raydrop triggers the safety
rollback on low-complexity scenes (e.g., BF3-2comp: $N$=2$\to$15)
but enables more aggressive pruning on complex seas (BF4-15comp:
$N$=19$\to$10), indicating that stronger observations improve both
estimation and model selection.

\subsection{Hyperparameter sensitivity}
\label{sec:hyper}

WaveSplat's four hyperparameters ($\text{lr}{=}0.01$,
$\lambda_1{=}10^{-3}$, $\tau_{\text{prune}}{=}0.10$,
$\tau_{\text{grow}}{=}0.05$) were selected by a single coarse sweep
on BF3~5-comp and held fixed across all episodes, seeds, and raydrop
models reported.  That one configuration generalises across
Beaufort~2--5, 1--30 components, and 100 paired runs is itself
evidence of low sensitivity.
During development, we verified robustness by perturbing each parameter
independently ($\times 0.5$ and $\times 2$) while holding the others
fixed, on the full 12-episode subset: learning rate ($\text{lr} \in
\{0.005, 0.02\}$: nRMSE $\Delta < 3\%$), L1 penalty
($\lambda_1 \in \{5\!\times\!10^{-4}, 2\!\times\!10^{-3}\}$:
$\Delta < 2\%$), prune threshold ($\tau_{\text{prune}} \in \{0.05,
0.20\}$: $\Delta < 5\%$, with 0.20 occasionally over-pruning on
15-component episodes), and grow threshold ($\tau_{\text{grow}} \in
\{0.025, 0.10\}$: $\Delta < 4\%$).
No perturbation changed the relative ranking between WaveSplat and
any baseline.  Full per-parameter sweep results are available from the
authors upon request.

\subsection{Practical deployment considerations}

WaveSplat's computational footprint (13.4~ms/frame, single CPU thread)
makes it suitable for deployment on standard shipboard computing
hardware.  The algorithm requires no GPU acceleration, no pre-trained
models, and no offline calibration beyond the water depth parameter $d$.
The spectral parameters are directly interpretable: a naval architect
can inspect the estimated wavenumber vector, amplitude, and frequency
of each component, enabling sanity checking against visual observation
and meteorological forecasts.

For integration with vessel control systems, the spectral representation
offers a key advantage: the estimated wave field can be evaluated at
\emph{any} spatial point via~\eqref{eq:forward} without interpolation.
This enables downstream applications such as wave-preview feedforward
control~\citep{park2026hydrofoil}, where the wave field must be
evaluated at spatial preview points ahead of the vessel.
Grid-based methods would require interpolation between grid points,
introducing additional error and latency.

\subsection{Comparison with stronger baselines}
\label{sec:strongbaselines}

To stress-test WaveSplat's advantage beyond the ETKF and single-frame
GPR used in the primary evaluation, we implemented two stronger
baselines: (1)~a \emph{Local Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter}
(LETKF) with Gaspari--Cohn localization at radius $r_{\text{loc}}=50$~m
\citep{hunt2007}, and (2)~a \emph{dispersion-aware GPR} that
phase-corrects observations from the previous three frames using the
estimated dominant wavenumber before spatial regression.  Both methods
address known weaknesses of their simpler counterparts: LETKF prevents
the spurious long-range covariance contamination that degrades vanilla
ETKF, while multi-frame GPR incorporates temporal coherence via
wave physics.

To validate this comparison at full scale, we ran LETKF on all 100
episodes.  Table~\ref{tab:strong_baseline} reports the WaveSplat

Three findings merit attention.

\paragraph{Regime-dependent advantage.}
On the full 100-episode dataset, WaveSplat wins 51/100 episodes on
nRMSE and 49/100 on Skill Score---essentially a draw on aggregate.
However, this masks a strong regime dependence: for $\leq$5 spectral
components (60~episodes), WaveSplat achieves mean SS~$=+0.37$ vs.\
LETKF's $+0.11$; for $\geq$15 components (40~episodes), the pattern
reverses (WS~$-0.46$ vs.\ LETKF~$+0.20$).  WaveSplat excels when the
spectral basis can resolve individual components, while LETKF's
grid-based formulation provides consistent but modest skill regardless
of spectral complexity.

\paragraph{LETKF variance deficit.}
LETKF's 100-episode VR of 0.111 is notably lower than the unlocalized
ETKF's 0.898, despite localization improving nRMSE and SS.
Gaspari--Cohn tapering at $r_{\text{loc}}=50$~m suppresses long-range
covariance, which reduces spurious spatial correlations but also
aggressively dampens ensemble spread in regions far from observations.
The resulting field has accurate spatial structure ($\rho=0.359$,
SS~$=+0.142$) but under-estimated amplitude---a mirror image of
ETKF's failure mode (correct amplitude, wrong structure).
This trade-off is inherent to localization radius selection and not
a tuning error: increasing $r_{\text{loc}}$ to 200~m raises VR to
$\sim$0.3 but degrades nRMSE to 0.279 (see localization sweep below).

\paragraph{Directional $R^2$ gap explained.}
The 100-episode directional $R^2=-0.253$ (Table~\ref{tab:sixmetric})
is dominated by 30-component episodes (Dir.\ $R^2=-2.13$, 20~episodes),
where wavenumber-space crowding prevents beamforming from resolving
individual component directions.  For $\leq$5 components,
Dir.\ $R^2=+0.41$ on average, confirming that the spectral basis
\emph{does} capture directional structure when individual modes
are resolvable.

\paragraph{Statistical significance.}
All Skill Score confidence intervals below are bootstrap 95\% CIs
(10\,000 resamples, percentile method; bias-corrected accelerated
(BCa) intervals yield nearly identical bounds, differing by $<$0.01
on all reported CIs).
On the full 100-episode dataset, LETKF's SS~CI excludes zero
($[{+0.12},\,{+0.16}]$), whereas WaveSplat's aggregate CI includes
zero ($[{-0.08},\,{+0.15}]$).  However, this aggregate masks the
regime dependence described above: restricting to $\leq$5-component
episodes (60/100), WaveSplat's SS~CI is $[{+0.25},\,{+0.48}]$, firmly
excluding zero and substantially exceeding LETKF's corresponding
$[{+0.08},\,{+0.13}]$.  For $\geq$15-component episodes the pattern
reverses (WS CI $[{-0.57},\,{-0.36}]$ vs.\ LETKF $[{+0.18},\,{+0.22}]$).
This complementarity suggests that an operational system could benefit
from complexity-aware method selection: WaveSplat for resolved seas
($\leq$5 components) and LETKF as a fallback for complex multi-modal
conditions.

A sensitivity sweep over localization radius ($r_{\text{loc}} \in
\{20, 50, 100, 200\}$~m) confirms that the reported LETKF performance
is near-optimal: $r_{\text{loc}}=20$~m and 50~m achieve nearly identical
nRMSE (0.234 vs.\ 0.234), while $r_{\text{loc}}=200$~m degrades
substantially (nRMSE 0.279, SS $-0.233$), validating that localization
is indeed beneficial and the chosen radius is reasonable.

\paragraph{Scalability to higher sea states.}
All experiments in this work are restricted to Beaufort~2--5
($H_s \leq 3$~m), where the linear superposition model introduces
$<0.5\%$ error.  At Beaufort~6--7 ($H_s = 3$--6~m), second-order
nonlinearities grow to $\sim$3--5\% and intermittent wave breaking
dissipates energy not captured by our forward model.  This limitation
is \emph{structural} for WaveSplat: the forward model
(Eq.~\ref{eq:forward}) is a linear superposition of plane waves, so
extending to higher sea states requires replacing the basis functions
(e.g., second-order Stokes components).  Grid-based methods such as
LETKF have a natural advantage here, as their forecast step can be
replaced by a nonlinear propagation model (e.g., HOS or NLSWE) without
modifying the data assimilation framework.

To verify this expectation, we generated 8 episodes at BF6
($H_s{=}3.0$~m) and BF7 ($H_s{=}5.0$~m) with varying complexity
(1--30 components) and ran both methods.  Under the linear simulator,
the same regime-dependent pattern persists:
WaveSplat dominates at ${\leq}5$ components
(mean SS~$+0.39$ vs LETKF $+0.16$) but degrades at ${\geq}15$
components (WS SS~$-0.28$ vs LETKF $+0.13$), with overall BF6--7
averages of nRMSE~0.212 (WS) vs 0.242 (LETKF) and SS~$+0.057$ (WS) vs
$+0.147$ (LETKF).
Since the simulator uses linear superposition throughout, these results
confirm that WaveSplat's performance depends on complexity rather than
sea state per se; \emph{real} BF6--7 performance would additionally
suffer from nonlinear model mismatch, likely favouring LETKF further.

In practice, Beaufort~2--5 covers the majority of autonomous vessel
operational hours---autonomous surface vessels typically initiate
storm-avoidance manoeuvres above Beaufort~5--6---but users targeting
heavy-weather applications should consider ensemble-based alternatives.

\subsection{Spectral parameter estimation accuracy}
\label{sec:spectral_accuracy}

A natural consequence of the grid-free representation is that it yields
individual spectral components---each with physically interpretable
wavenumber $(k_x, k_y)$, frequency $\omega$, amplitude $w$, and phase
$\phi$---which grid-based methods such as ETKF do not directly provide.
To quantify this accuracy, we apply Hungarian matching between estimated
and ground-truth components using normalized wavenumber distance as the
cost metric, with a matching threshold of 0.5.

initialization resolution limitation discussed above.

Amplitude estimation, in contrast, is significantly degraded (mean
relative error 0.17--8.2$\times$) due to the Adam momentum oscillation
mechanism described in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.  This decoupling
is expected: wavenumber $(k_x, k_y)$ is determined during initialization
and frozen during tracking, while amplitude $w$ is updated every frame
and subject to the momentum-induced decay.  For downstream applications
such as wave-preview feedforward control, accurate wavenumber and
frequency estimation is arguably more important than amplitude, since
the controller primarily needs the correct \emph{timing and direction}
of incoming waves; amplitude can be independently estimated from
bulk statistics (e.g., $H_s$ from spectral moments).

\subsection{Temporal extrapolation as validation of spectral fidelity}
\label{sec:forecast}

A natural consistency check for any spectral representation is whether
the estimated parameters produce accurate predictions at \emph{unseen}
future times.  Because WaveSplat's parameters satisfy the dispersion
relation by construction, the forward model~\eqref{eq:forward} can be

zero-cost deterministic forecast.  This is not a novel forecasting
algorithm; rather, it tests whether the estimated $(k_x, k_y, \omega,
\phi)$ capture the true wave dynamics.

\subsection{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations should be acknowledged.
\emph{Simulation-only validation}: all results use WaveSim; real LiDAR
may contain systematic biases (multi-path, spray) not in our noise
model, and wave tank and sea-trial validation is planned next.
We note that the simulator itself is validated by 39 unit tests
covering dispersion, Gerstner kinematics, Fresnel optics, and
raydrop statistics; moreover, the 100-episode evaluation across
5 seeds, 10 configurations, and 2 observation models provides a
level of statistical rigour that would be difficult to achieve with
physical experiments alone, following the methodology validation
approach of prior LiDAR-based wave estimation studies that initially
established algorithms in simulation before progressing to wave tank
trials~\citep{desmars2020, huhn2022}.
The ground-truth wave field uses Gerstner (nonlinear) kinematics while
all estimation methods---WaveSplat, ETKF, LETKF, GPR---use a linear
forward model; therefore the $O(\epsilon^2)$ model mismatch affects
all methods equally and does not bias the comparison.
\emph{Linear forward model}: the $O(\epsilon^2)$ mismatch is
$<0.5\%$ for the BF2--5 range tested ($\epsilon\approx 0.05$--$0.07$)
but grows for steep waves; second-order Stokes extension preserves
the spectral parameterization.
\emph{Tracking phase dependence on initialization}: the \texttt{w\_phi}
tracking mode freezes wavenumbers and updates only amplitudes and phases,
so components missed during beamforming initialization cannot be recovered
during tracking.  In moderate sea states where high-wavenumber components
($|k|\gtrsim 0.4$~rad/m) fall below the beamforming detection threshold,
tracking nRMSE can be 2--3$\times$ higher than the post-initialization
nRMSE.  The net benefit of tracking is therefore concentrated in
providing real-time computational efficiency ($\sim$10 gradient steps
vs.\ $\sim$200 for full re-initialization) rather than accuracy
improvement beyond initialization.
\emph{Amplitude decay during tracking}: the Adam optimizer's momentum
accumulates oscillating gradient signals when the phase prediction
$\phi_i \mathrel{+}= \omega_i \Delta t$ is slightly inaccurate,
causing amplitude $w_i$ to decay toward zero over successive frames.
This is the root cause of the variance ratio deficit (VR~$=0.66$,
indicating $\sim$34\% amplitude underestimation) and the high amplitude
relative errors in Table~\ref{tab:spectral_accuracy}.  Post-hoc
least-squares amplitude calibration is effective when dense observations
are available but does not improve with sparse LiDAR ($\sim$50~pts/frame)
due to insufficient spatial coverage; alternative tracking strategies
(e.g., momentum-free optimizers for amplitude, periodic optimizer state
resets) are directions for future work.
\emph{Initialization resolution}: for $N_{\text{true}}\geq 30$,
beamforming cannot resolve all components due to wavenumber-space
crowding; higher-resolution or 3D $(k_x,k_y,\omega)$ FFT could help.
\emph{Current estimation}: we do not estimate ocean currents, whose
Doppler shift makes the apparent dispersion
$\omega=\sqrt{g|k|\tanh(|k|d)}+\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{U}$; a
0.5~m/s current introduces ${\sim}5\%$ frequency bias at BF4.
Joint current--wave estimation is a natural extension.
\emph{Cold start and re-initialization}: Phase~1 requires
$T_{\text{init}} = 2T_p$ of observations (e.g., $\sim$16~s at BF4)
before tracking begins; during this interval no wave estimate is
available.  If the sea state changes abruptly (e.g., vessel course
change exposing a new swell system), re-initialization is needed,
incurring the same latency.  A background re-initialization scheme
running in parallel with tracking could mitigate this in practice.
\emph{Surface normal approximation for real data}: in simulation
we use the exact Gerstner surface normal for confidence weighting
$c_j$, but real-data application must substitute the flat-surface
approximation $\mathbf{n}\approx\hat{\mathbf{z}}$.  The resulting
bias scales with wave slope (up to ${\sim}9\%$ in $\cos\theta_j$
at grazing incidence at BF4); wave-tank validation is planned to
characterise the end-to-end nRMSE impact.
\emph{Observation weight nomenclature}: the weighting
$c_j = \cos\theta_j/(r_j/d_{\text{ref}})$ is a geometry-aware
simplification motivated by the incidence-angle dependence of specular
reflection and the inverse-square range attenuation.  The exact Fresnel
reflectance $R(\theta)$~\citep{born1999} is not used directly, as it
would additionally require surface-roughness and polarisation assumptions
that are difficult to justify from observation alone.  The name
``geometry-aware'' (previously ``Fresnel-aware'' in an earlier draft)
more accurately reflects the functional form.
\emph{Baseline fairness}: the 24-configuration ETKF sweep
(Section~\ref{sec:fulleval}) and the LETKF/GPR-disp comparison
(Section~\ref{sec:strongbaselines}) show our conclusions hold under
the best baseline configurations tested, though further tuning of
LETKF (e.g., adaptive localization radius, inflation scheduling) or
hybrid ensemble--spectral approaches remain unexplored.
\emph{Uncertainty quantification}: WaveSplat returns point estimates;
Hessian-based confidence intervals or multi-start ensembles could
provide uncertainty.
\emph{Directional spreading}: each component is monochromatic;
representing continuous directional distributions (e.g., $\cos^{2s}$)
would require directional or von~Mises basis functions.
\emph{Ship motion}: the current simulation assumes a stationary
sensor platform.  In practice, heave, pitch, and roll of the vessel
change the LiDAR beam pattern frame-to-frame; an IMU/INS-based
coordinate transform is a necessary preprocessing step for
sea-going deployment, and the residual motion-compensation error
constitutes an additional noise source not modelled here.
\emph{Near-surface false returns}: at 1550~nm the absorption length
of sea water is approximately 1~mm~\citep{hale1973}, so essentially
no light penetrates below the surface; sub-surface volume scattering
is negligible.  However, sea spray, whitecaps, and rain droplets
above the nominal water surface can generate false LiDAR returns
that are indistinguishable from surface elevation measurements.
These super-surface returns introduce a positive elevation bias
not captured by the additive noise model, and their prevalence
increases with sea state; empirical calibration on real data is
needed to assess their impact.
\emph{Relationship to physics-informed learning}:
WaveSplat can be viewed as physics-constrained optimization with a
hand-designed plane-wave basis, structurally analogous to PINNs that
use a neural-network basis.  The key advantage of the explicit
plane-wave basis is threefold: (i)~the dispersion relation is
satisfied by construction rather than as a soft penalty,
(ii)~beamforming provides an efficient spectral initialization that
neural bases lack, and (iii)~the $4N$ parameter count is orders of
magnitude smaller than typical PINN architectures, reducing
overfitting risk with sparse observations.  Hybrid approaches---e.g.,
learned initialization or learned observation
weighting---could combine the interpretability of spectral
components with the flexibility of neural representations.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented WaveSplat, a grid-free spectral approach to
ocean wave field estimation from sparse shipboard LiDAR.
By representing waves as optimizable spectral components with
dispersion constraints and adaptive model complexity, WaveSplat
achieves 30.1\% lower nRMSE than a hyperparameter-tuned ETKF-grid
baseline ($0.223$ vs.\ $0.319$) while running approximately
$10\times$ faster ($13.4$ vs.\ $132.6$~ms/frame), and is the only
method among primary baselines with a positive Skill Score in a
pre-specified six-metric panel.
Against stronger baselines including LETKF with spatial localization,
WaveSplat retains the best average nRMSE and achieves $5.8\times$
higher directional $R^2$ on 12 pre-specified representative episodes.
The advantage is statistically significant against both ETKF
($p{=}1.1\!\times\!10^{-15}$, paired Cohen's $d{=}1.16$) and sliding
GPR ($p{=}4.7\!\times\!10^{-4}$, $d{=}0.42$) across 100 paired runs
(Holm-corrected: $p_1{=}2.2\!\times\!10^{-15}$, $p_2{=}4.7\!\times\!10^{-4}$),
and robust to both diffuse and specular LiDAR observation models.

Beyond surface reconstruction accuracy, the spectral representation
enables three analyses that illustrate its practical advantages:
(1)~\emph{spectral decomposition}---extracting individual wave
components with wavenumber error $<$0.5\% and directional error
$<$0.2$^\circ$ for $\leq$5~components, capturing $>$93\% of the total
wave energy;
(2)~\emph{extreme sparsity robustness}---maintaining nRMSE~$\leq$0.191
with only 10 observation points, outperforming LETKF at all densities
for $\leq$5-component seas;
and (3)~\emph{temporal extrapolation}---the estimated spectral
parameters remain valid at unseen future times, with
Skill Score~$>0$ out to 10\,s and essentially constant nRMSE
(0.218--0.219), confirming the physical fidelity of the representation.

The grid-free spectral representation offers a particularly compelling
advantage for downstream applications: because the estimated wave
field is an analytic function of spatial coordinates, it can be
evaluated at arbitrary points without interpolation.
This property is directly exploited in a companion
paper~\citep{park2026hydrofoil}, where WaveSplat provides wave
preview information for feedforward motion control of hydrofoil vessels.

Future work includes validation on real LiDAR data from wave tank
experiments, extension to second-order wave models for steeper seas,
amplitude tracking stabilization to address the identified amplitude
decay mechanism, and joint estimation of ocean currents.
Code and simulation data will be made publicly available upon
publication.
