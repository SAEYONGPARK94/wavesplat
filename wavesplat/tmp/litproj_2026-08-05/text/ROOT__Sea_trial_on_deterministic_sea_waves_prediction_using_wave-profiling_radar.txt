ABSTRACT 
1 
Short-term deterministic sea wave prediction (DSWP) can facilitate the safe implementation 
2 
of a number of maritime operations. Wave profile radars, based on the conventional X-band 
3 
radars, have recently been proven to provide a low-cost convenient source of two-dimensional 
4 
wave measurement for DSWP. The Golden Arrow sea trial in the North Atlantic was dedicated 
5 
to collecting wave and vessel motion data for DSWP purposes, particularly under the large sea 
6 
conditions that are of practical interest in marine operations. The field data were collected using 
7 
a wave profiling radar and other measurement technologies for validation. This paper reports 
8 
on the successful application of a recently developed technique by Al-Ani et al. in DSWP to 
9 
the dedicated sea trial. 
10 
Keywords - Deterministic sea wave prediction (DSWP); quiescent period prediction (QPP); 
11 
sea trial; wave profiling radar 
12 
1.  Introduction 
13 
Short-term deterministic sea wave prediction (DSWP) can potentially play a useful role in 
14 
extending the sea states under which many critical maritime operations can be safely 
15 
undertaken (Crossland et al., 2009; Giron-Sierra and Esteban, 2010; Ferrier et al., 2013). 
16 
DSWP as a branch of marine science had its roots in the early 1990’s and has recently evolved 
17 
into a rapidly growing discipline, an illustrative sample of the literature being Morris et al. 
18 
(1992), Pourzanjani et al. (1992), Blondel et al. (2008), Belmont et al. (2014), Hilmer and 
19 
Thrinhill (2014), and Connell et al. (2015). Unlike traditional statistical estimation of sea wave 
20 
properties (Tucker and Pitt, 2001), the discipline of DSWP aims to predict (few tens of seconds 
21 
in advance) the actual profile of the sea surface and its evolution. Typical prediction timescales 
22 
are in the range of 60-120 s ahead. Two common elements make DSWP an attractive tool in 
23 
safely operating many launch and recovery tasks including: the launch and recovery of fixed 
24 

2 
 
and rotary wing aircraft; small, surface or submersible vehicles, such as RHIBs; and cargo, 
1 
fuel, and personnel transfer systems between vessels, and vessels onto offshore structures. 
2 
Firstly, while the overall execution of such tasks may take a significant amount of time, the 
3 
key wave-height-critical sub-tasks that actually limit the sea state under which they can be 
4 
carried out are short, typically less than one minute.  Secondly, a fundamental property of most 
5 
sea conditions encountered that are of relevance to ships is that sets of large waves alternate 
6 
with sets of smaller waves, the exception being highly multidirectional large wind-wave seas 
7 
encountered within very strong storm systems when the wind conditions themselves typically 
8 
prohibit most launch and recovery operations. The smaller waves present in otherwise large 
9 
seas are, by definition, of lower height than the standard sea statistics for the prevailing 
10 
conditions, and consequently the intervals in which these lower height waves occur are referred 
11 
to as quiescent periods.  Combining the above two elements, i.e. the relatively short duration 
12 
of the wave-height critical sub-tasks and the presence of quiescent periods, suggests that if the 
13 
smaller waves could be deterministically predicted then a range of maritime operations could 
14 
be safely undertaken under conditions whose overall sea state statistics would prohibit the 
15 
execution of the task.  Hence, there is an increasing interest in the capabilities, such as quiescent 
16 
period prediction (QPP), that are based upon DSWP (Pourzanjani et al., 1992; Ferrier et al., 
17 
2000; Crossland et al., 2009; Ferrier et al., 2013). 
18 
As discussed in Belmont et al. (2014), wave profiling radars are the only realistic sensing 
19 
technology for shipborne applications of DSWP.  Conventional vessel-mounted wave radar 
20 
returns statistical wave data (sea surface roughness).  In contrast, the OceanWaveS WaMoS II 
21 
wave profiling radar (Reichert et al., 1999) offers the prospect of measuring the actual sea 
22 
surface profile over an area of a few square kilometres around a vessel, based on the standard 
23 
X-band radar carried by ships for safe navigation purposes. In Hilmer and Thornhill (2014), 
24 
the accuracy of WaMoS II at rendering sea surface elevation was evaluated against a wave 
25 

3 
 
buoy, where the majority of correlation coefficients exceeded 0.87. Due to the commercial 
1 
nature of the source, full details of all the processing algorithms employed in extracting wave 
2 
profiles from radar backscatter data are not available to users and their details are 
3 
understandably not fully disclosed in the company’s published material (Hilmer and Thornhill 
4 
2015).  
5 
Using experimental wave profiling radar, Belmont et al. (2014) explored two forms of 
6 
DSWP techniques. The first was so called “multiple fixed point” system based upon setting up 
7 
a system of equations that described a sea composed of a modest number of wave directions. 
8 
This approach had some success. Secondly a fully two-dimensional method in the form classic 
9 
discrete time Fourier transform was employed that was slightly less effective. The WaMoS II 
10 
system was deployed during a sea trial, designated as Golden Arrow, undertaken expressly to 
11 
gather data for DSWP purposes. A fundamental challenge inherent in the use of WaMoS II is 
12 
that it employs standard navigation radars whose antenna are typically mechanically rotated 
13 
with scan times of few seconds. This means that the radar backscatter data evolves in both time 
14 
and space and hence simple spatial Fourier techniques are only valid where the scan period is 
15 
very small compared to the shortest period of interest. In the Golden Arrow trial, the rotational 
16 
period of 2.52 s is considerable compared to the dominant wave periods of 11 s and 13 s for 
17 
the prevailing westerly and south-westerly wave systems.  
18 
In order to address the above problem, a new two-dimensional approach was developed in 
19 
Al-Ani et al. (2019). This technique can accept the mixed space-time data arising from the 
20 
rotating radar antenna and furthermore is not susceptible to the condition number problems that 
21 
usually arise with any form of inversion into prediction models (e.g., Alford et al. (2015), 
22 
Belmont et al. (2014) and Connell et al. (2015)). When applied to simulated wave data, the new 
23 
technique produces very promising results (Al-Ani et al., 2019). In the present report, the 
24 

4 
 
authors focus on applying this approach to the field data of the Golden Arrow and report on 
1 
the wave prediction accuracy achieved.  
2 
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. In Section 2, the authors summarise the 
3 
key elements of the new technique. The performance metrics that are used to assess the quality 
4 
of prediction are detailed in Section 3. In Section 4, we describe the sea trial data and illustrate 
5 
the accuracy of the wave profiles prediction. In Section 5, conclusions are drawn.

4. The Sea Trial and Results  
12 
The illustrative data presented here was obtained during an interval of long-period high sea 
13 
state during the Golden Arrow sea trial on the 24th November 2014 between 9:20 and 14:00 
14 
UTC off the western coast of Scotland, approximately 100 km west of the Isle of Mull. The 
15 
development of the wave system during this part of the trial as reflected by the vessel’s wave 
16 
driven heave motion is shown in Fig. 1, where the standard deviation of the ship heave is 
17 
calculated over a window of ten minutes.   
18 
Statistical directional wave spectra were determined from both: the WaMoS II wave radar 
19 
(operating in traditional statistical mode as opposed to wave profiling mode) and free floating 
20 
directional multi-axis wave buoys. Both data sources indicated that there were two significant 
21 
wave directions, one from the west and one from the south west. (Here the wave directions 
22 
represent the direction the waves are propagating from.) The dominant periods of these two 
23 

9 
 
wave systems were up to 11 s and 13 s, respectively. These timescales were significant as the 
1 
3dB point of the vessel frequency response in heave corresponds to 9 s period waves. Thus, to 
2 
a zeroth-order approximation, the vessel acted as a wave follower in heave as will be discussed 
3 
later. Between 13:00 and 14:00 UTC the ship was manoeuvring slowly and the waves were at 
4 
their highest, and therefore the study is focused in this hour. The speed, course and heading of 
5 
the ship from 12:58 to 14:00 UTC is shown in Fig. 2.

4.1 Wave profiles predictions 
7 
A subdomain from each WaMoS II scan is chosen, where the measurements are most 
8 
reliable. The size and location of the reliable measurement zone, also known as the “sweet 
9 
spot” in the literature, depends on the sea waves properties, radar height and characteristics, 
10 
and the prediction requirements. It is usually located in the directions where waves are traveling 
11 
towards the radar, beyond the dead zone around the ship where the radar backscatter is 
12

13 
et al. (2016) for a discussion). For easy illustration, we restrict the measurement zone here to a 
14 
trapezium, west of the ship. The dimensions and location of the measurement trapezium are 
15 
illustrated in Fig.3 a.  The axes in Fig. 3 are labelled according to the ship location that is set 
16

4.2 The vessel heave motion prediction 
1 
Most applications of DSWP will involve determining the predicted wave-induced motions 
2 
of some floating body. In linear ship theory, vessels are typically modelled by 6 weakly-
3 
coupled ordinary linear differential equations that are typically Fourier transformed to produce 
4 
a system of Frequency Response functions that are acted upon by the Fourier Transform of the 
5 
wave system. Alternatively (and equivalently), the response of a narrow strip of vessel to an 
6 
impulsive wave force input is integrated over the vessel hull profile (strip theory), giving rise 
7 
to the so called response amplitude operators (RAO) coefficients. In either case, below 
8 
resonance, the resulting heave Frequency Response function is crudely speaking a low pass 
9 
filter. Consequently, at low wave frequencies relative to the ship’s dynamics as prevail in the 
10 
present situation (the resonance is at approximately a 9 s period with dominant wave systems 
11 
centred on 11 s and 13 s), the heave transfer function is at least to a zeroth-order approximation 
12 
constant. Similarly, the phase shift between wave forcing functions and vessel motion is again 
13 
to a zeroth-order approximation zero. Hence, for heave motions, the vessel approximately 
14 
follows the wave profile and to a first-order approximation there is no requirement to 
15 
compensate for vessel motions. This is not the case in pitch where the response changes rapidly 
16 
with frequency below resonance which is why only heave is considered here. 
17 
A high precision vessel motion sensor was present on the vessel. However, this was mounted 
18 
14 m forward and 2 m above the vessels nominal dynamic centre (as supplied by the vessel 
19 
operators), with the vessel being 90 m in length.  The sensor’s location meant that other modes 
20 
of motion made some contributions to the measured heave. Unfortunately the vessel had been 
21 
“cut and extended” and in addition had considerable extraneous deck equipment all of which 
22 
meant that the exact location of the motion centre was not well defined. This made it difficult 
23 
to confidently decouple pure heave motion so the authors were forced to use the uncorrected 
24 
motion senor data as a “raw heave” motion estimate. Thus, Fig. 8 shows the correlations 
25 

13 
 
between the wave predictions (taken to represent heave) and the raw measured vessel heave 
1 
motion that was available to us in the time span 13:21 – 13:43 UTC, for prediction horizons 
2 
[𝜏𝑠 ,𝜏𝑠+ 60] s and [𝜏𝑠 ,𝜏𝑠+ 120] s . Given the very crude nature of the heave assessment, the 
3 
results shown are surprisingly good.  
4 
The drops in the prediction quality seen in Fig. 8 appear to correlate to some extent with 
5 
changes in the vessel’s heading and speed. Fortunately, the onset and the return to good 
6 
prediction occur over a sufficiently long timescale for them to be recognised during operations. 
7 
These drops are not present in the simulation work in Al-Ani et al. (2019) and as with the wave 
8 
prediction estimates they would appear to be associated with the actual wave profile 
9 
measurements of real seas rather than being fundamental artefacts of the prediction technique. 
10 
5. Conclusions 
11 
The level of errors encountered in the prediction based on the data obtained in the Golden 
12 
Arrow sea trial indicates that in applications such as QPP (e.g., for operations such as launch 
13 
and recovery of small craft and air vehicles), DSWP has real potential as a practical maritime 
14 
tool. Drops occur in the prediction accuracy whose detailed cause remains a subject for further 
15 
work. The absence of these in simulation studies indicates they are associated with aspects of 
16 
the wave radar determination of actual sea profiles. However, from an application perspective, 
17 
the onset of these occurs sufficiently slowly for them to be easily identified and thus not 
18 
represent a barrier for future operations exploiting DSWP.
